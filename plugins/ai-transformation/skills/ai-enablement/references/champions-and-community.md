Read this on entering step 4. It designs the people who carry the program into each group (champions), the places
where people get help and show their work (clinics, office hours, spotlights), and the community that keeps it going.

## 1. Goal and exit criteria

**Goal.** Social proof and help close to the work. Generative AI is discretionary: unlike a leave-booking system, people
can keep working the old way [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026], so use has to be earned through value people can see, clear
expectations and respected peers using it (advisor's reading).

**Exit when all of these are true:**
- **Selection criteria** for champions, and how they are invited (the user chooses the people).
- **A champion role card:** time per week, deliverable, forum duties, recognition, reporting line, term.
- **Numbers per group**, recorded as a hypothesis to test, not a ratio known to work.
- **Forums** with their rhythm, owner and audience: spotlights, clinics, office hours, a community of practice.
- **Reach to the not-keen:** how forums reach segments that do not come on their own.
- **Routes out of forums:** good workflows go to the library (step 5); failures go to the central team; blocked uses
  go to governance.

## 2. How to run it

1. **Agree the criteria first** (H1), before anyone suggests names. Choose for workflow credibility and standing in
   the group, not AI expertise [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026; LAW360-2025].
2. **Find candidates with data** (H2): staff surveys, help-desk patterns and practice personas, as Michael Best did
   [LAW360-2025]. The step 1 builders segment is a starting list.
3. **Write the role card** (§4) and agree the time with the champions' managers.
4. **Design the forums** (§5): a fixed rhythm, mixed seniority on stage, clinics for real problems.
5. **Plan reach** to skeptics and blocked groups (H6), and link to step 6.
6. **Write it** into `program.md` and add forum measures to `measures.yaml` if wanted (attendance is reach, not depth).

**Rule 1 in practice.** Help the user set criteria; the user picks the people. Record champions by group and role
("two senior managers, audit Leeds"); a name may appear only as the owner of a champion deliverable if the user
offers it. Never rank champions or users, and recognise teams rather than publish individual league tables.

## 4. The champion role

**Role card fields:** group; role level; time per week; deliverable; forum duties; reporting line; recognition; term;
the route for what they learn (library, central team, governance).

**What firms report** (firm-reported; no comparison groups):
- Womble Bond Dickinson champions commit roughly 2-3 hours a week and each completes a capstone, a custom agent or
  workflow for their practice group; more than 150 have joined since early 2026 (undated firm page)
  [WBD-CHAMPIONS-ND].
- Lewis Silkin's champions need not be the most advanced users; they need to know where the tool fits existing
  workflows, and many are knowledge lawyers [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026]. Each team has a lead who works with champions to
  find use cases [HARVEY-LEWISSILKIN-2025].
- Ropes & Gray reports over 60 champions and growth from 32 users to about 2,200 (firm news page citing the Wall
  Street Journal) [ROPES-WSJ-2026].
- Duane Morris names practice-group AI champions (a partner, counsel or senior associate with early access who tests
  tools against real work) (`professional-services-talent-learning-02`, grade B).
- Firms package know-how through legal engineers and practice teams who build playbooks and agents
  (`professional-services-work-production-17`, grade B).
- PwC reports over 3,200 AI champions firm-wide, alongside a US AI tax leader and a governance committee
  (`tax-research-advisory-07`, grade B).

**Numbers.** No source gives a champion-to-user ratio with outcomes. Treat any ratio as a hypothesis (for example,
Ropes & Gray's over 60 champions for about 2,200 users, outcome not isolated) and review it in step 7.

## 5. Forums: spotlights, clinics, office hours, community

| Forum | Purpose | Design points |
|---|---|---|
| **Spotlight** | Social proof: people show real uses | Three or four speakers from every level; Lewis Silkin's ran from junior marketing managers to IP disputes partners, because "a trainee and a partner" saying what they did carries weight [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026]. Senior leaders front it: Lewis Silkin's Joint Managing Partners led the spotlights [HARVEY-LEWISSILKIN-2025] |
| **Junior-to-partner showcase** | Make juniors visible; partners learn | In Ropes & Gray's registered funds practice two first-years present AI uses to partners each month [BLAW-ROPES-2026] |
| **Clinic** | Fix a specific problem on real work | Bring a task; leave with a working approach or a library candidate |
| **Office hours** | A safe place for basic questions | Specialists available, and recognition for experimenters [LEXIS-CONSULT-2025]; useful for senior people who do not want to look unskilled |
| **Competition** | Harvest real workflow problems | Ropes & Gray's 25 teams were judged on problem-solving, feasibility, appropriate AI use and presentation, with full billable credit; its knowledge director said teams focused on processes they are involved in [BLAW-ROPES-2026] |
| **Community of practice** | Keep learning between events | People who share a concern and improve by interacting regularly; needs a shared domain, a community that interacts regularly, and a shared practice (tools, stories, ways of handling recurring problems) [WENGER-ND] |

**Cadence.** Pick a fixed rhythm and keep it; Lewis Silkin calls its communications cadence a "drumbeat"
[HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026]. State the rhythm (for example, monthly spotlight, weekly clinic) as a habit, not a deadline.

**Psychological safety.** Teams learn from new practice when people can try things and admit errors without penalty
(pre-AI field study) [EDMONDSON-1999]. Partners and senior specialists risk their standing if they make a visible
mistake with AI; private sessions and peer settings lower that cost (advisor's judgment).

## 6. Expert moves

| Move | When | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Pick champions for workflow knowledge and standing, not AI skill | Selection | Who can translate uses into the group's actual matters [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026] |
| Find influencers with data, not nominations | Selection | Vocal people open to improving things, who need not be tech-savvy [LAW360-2025] |
| Make champion duty explicit: hours, a deliverable, a forum | Role design | Whether the role is real [WBD-CHAMPIONS-ND] |
| Run short spotlights with speakers from every level | Community | That peers persuade better than central instruction [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026] |
| Use a competition to harvest real workflow problems, with judging criteria | Use cases are thin | Candidate workflows and enthusiasm [BLAW-ROPES-2026] |
| Pair a respected peer with a group of skeptics for repeated hands-on sessions | Broadcast training stalled | Trust and habit barriers. Diffusion is "essentially a social process" (Gawande, citing Rogers; health-care example) [GAWANDE-2013] |
| Accept that early adopters are scarce in a low-risk-tolerance profession | Forming the first group | Whether to lean on trusted staff rather than volunteers [LAW360-2025] |
| Ask "which tasks will you declare the default way, and who signs that off?" | After early wins | Whether adoption is turning into standard practice; Lewis Silkin's aim is for the tool to be "the standard way the work gets done" for tasks such as tribunal chronologies [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026] |

## 7. Checklist and decision rules

**Checklist:** selection criteria; time and recognition agreed with managers; kick-off and community charter;
spotlight rhythm with mixed seniority; clinics for specific problems; office hours; route to the library; route to
governance for blocked uses; communications rhythm.

**Decision rules**
- Choose by workflow credibility and influence, not by being the most advanced user [HARVEY-CHAMPIONS-2026].
- Include juniors in showcases to partners [BLAW-ROPES-2026].
- No protected time, no champion: if managers will not release the hours, record the risk.
- Recognise teams and workflows, not individuals ranked by usage (core rule 1).
- Say plainly that no published study measures what champion networks add to sustained use; the firm's own step 7
  measures are the test.

## 8. Common traps

| Trap | How to avoid it |
|---|---|
| Champions chosen for enthusiasm alone | Criteria first: workflow credibility and standing |
| Champions with no time or deliverable | Role card agreed with their managers |
| Forums that only reach the keen | Plan reach to skeptics and blocked groups (step 6) |
| Only enthusiasts present | Mixed seniority; juniors to partners |
| League tables of named people | Team-level recognition (core rule 1) |
| Cadence that lapses when work is busy | A rhythm the senior sponsor attends |

## 9. Worked example

*Illustrative; the firm and figures are invented.* The accounting firm sets criteria: respected in their group for
how work is done, willing to show unfinished work, able to explain to partners. The user picks two or three people
per practice and office; the file records "audit: two senior managers and one senior, Leeds; tax: one manager and one
senior, York". Role card: two hours a week, recorded against targets; one capstone workflow each, which goes through
the library test (step 5); one clinic a month; a term of one cycle, then rotate. Forums: a monthly firmwide
spotlight led by the managing partner, with a junior and a partner on stage each time; weekly clinics per practice;
office hours run by the central team for partners who prefer a private session. Reach: audit partners get a separate
session on their own engagement files (step 6). Number of champions is recorded as a hypothesis to review in step 7.
