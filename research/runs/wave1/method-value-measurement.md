# Method topic: value-measurement-business-cases

Stream M (method). Research date: 2026-10-16. Phases fed: prioritize, business-case, plan-and-control.

Topic focus: how to size, prioritize and measure AI opportunities credibly: baselining, value levers (capacity, speed, quality, growth, cost, risk, experience), hours-released vs realized value, ranges and assumptions, scoring and prioritization frameworks, quick wins vs foundations vs big bets, total cost of AI, KPI design and tracking, benefit realization, overclaiming traps, and how organizations measure AI ROI in 2025–2026.

Conventions used below:

- Source refs are `PUBLISHER-SHORT-YYYY` and resolve in section 9. Locators are sections, headings or table names, because most sources are web pages without page numbers.
- Provenance tags on figures: `self-reported` (survey or interview respondents describing their own organization), `vendor-published` (a vendor or consultancy publishing its own or a commissioned study), `independent` (academic, government or third-party measurement), `model-estimated` (an AI model produced the estimate), `prediction` (an analyst forecast, not a measurement).
- `search-only` means I reached the figure only through search snippets or secondary coverage and could not open the primary document. Those figures are listed in section 6 under a separate heading and are not used to support any expert move.
- Items tagged `(synthesis)` are my own working synthesis of several sources, offered as a heuristic and not as something one source states.

## 1. Summary

- **Hours released are not value realized.** The best-documented public trials (UK government Copilot experiment, UK Department for Business and Trade) show self-reported time savings; the DBT evaluation says it found no evidence that time savings led to improved productivity, and the cross-government report says it could not identify how the saved time was spent (UKGDS-2025 §6; UKDBT-2025 Exec Summary). The advisor should always ask what the freed time turns into before putting a value on it.
- **Self-report overstates, and rework eats a large share.** In the METR randomized trial, developers expected a 24% speedup and, after tasks that took 19% longer, still believed they had been 20% faster (METR-2025). Workday's survey says nearly 40% of AI time savings are lost to rework (WORKDAY-2026). Net-of-review time, not gross time, is the number to size.
- **Most organizations cannot yet tie AI to P&L.** Across 2025–2026 surveys, only a minority report enterprise-level financial impact: for example 25% of AI initiatives delivering expected ROI in IBM's CEO study (IBM-2025), 15% of AI decision-makers reporting an EBITDA lift in Forrester's data (FORRESTER-2026), and 5% of firms getting AI value at scale in BCG's study (BCG-2025). These are self-reported, differ in definition, and should be quoted with what they measure, never as a generic "failure rate".
- **What the higher-return groups do differently is measured outcomes plus redesigned work.** BCG reports that more than 60% of "future-built" firms rigorously track AI value versus 17% of "stagnating" firms (BCG-2025). Wharton's 2026 survey reports 35% strongly positive ROI among leaders tracking at least one growth measure versus 13% tracking efficiency alone, and describes this as an association in self-reported data (WHARTON-2026). Gartner's finance work reports that "Upend" initiatives (new value propositions, products or markets) were more than twice as likely to report high realized value (GARTNER-CFO2-2026).
- **Size with ranges, baselines and a counterfactual.** Practitioner methods converge on a few habits: baseline before the pilot, isolate the effect of the change (control group, staggered rollout or estimate adjustment), express uncertain inputs as ranges, and spend measurement effort only on variables whose uncertainty could change the decision (ROIINST-2014 §Phillips; HDR-AIE; NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023).
- **Total cost is more than tokens, and it is moving.** Unit prices at a fixed capability level have fallen fast (9x to 900x per year depending on the benchmark milestone, per Epoch), yet the FinOps Foundation argues that enterprise consumption, reasoning and agentic workloads are growing faster than unit savings, and KPMG reports that only 26% of organizations have full real-time visibility into AI operating cost (EPOCH-2025; FINOPS-2026; KPMG-2026). A business case needs a cost stack and a consumption forecast, not a price per seat.
- **Macro and micro evidence both counsel modest, staged expectations.** Task-level savings translate into process- and economy-level gains only in proportion to the share of work affected (NBER-ACEMOGLU-2024); a Danish register-data study finds precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours two years in, alongside task reorganization (NBER-HUMLUM-2025); and the J-curve literature says complementary intangible investment precedes measured gains (NBER-JCURVE-2020).
- **Evidence quality is the main limit.** Almost every outcome figure is self-reported or vendor-published. Independent measurement exists for a few settings (customer support, software development, UK public sector) and is rarely about professional-services work. The advisor should say so plainly to users.

## 2. Core concepts in plain language

**2.1 Hours released vs value realized.** A tool that saves a person 26 minutes a day (the self-reported UK cross-government average, UKGDS-2025 §5) has *released capacity*. Value arrives only if that capacity is redeployed to something the business wants (more client work, faster cycle, higher quality), or is removed as cost (fewer contractors, fewer agency fees, avoided hires). In knowledge work, hours are often absorbed by more email, more review, or more output of the same kind. The Gartner HR release reports that only 7% of surveyed organizations provide guidelines on how to use time saved by AI (GARTNER-HR-2025, Optimize Time Freed Up by AI). For AI, the advisor's job is to turn "hours" into a named destination with an owner.

**2.2 Value levers.** The advisor's working taxonomy is seven levers: capacity, speed, quality, growth, cost, risk and experience `(synthesis)`. Individual levers appear across the sources: Gartner's sales Q&A describes ROI moving from user-level efficiency to "capacity, coverage, conversion or revenue growth" as use cases mature (GARTNER-CSO-2026); Deloitte's survey lists productivity and efficiency, insights and decision-making, cost, customer relationships, product innovation and revenue as realized benefits (DELOITTE-2026); EY's survey lists where leaders reinvest gains (EY-2025). Each lever needs a different metric and a different level of proof: cost and capacity can be baselined from operating data, quality needs a defined error or rework measure, growth needs an attribution method, risk needs an expected-loss view, and experience needs a survey instrument. For knowledge work, quality and risk are frequently the real value and are the levers most often left out of business cases.

**2.3 Baseline and counterfactual.** A pilot result means little without "compared with what". The Phillips method names this step "isolating the effects of the program" and says excluding it produces invalid ROI calculations (ROIINST-2014 §Comparison). Good designs in the sources include a staggered rollout with a comparison group (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023), randomized assignment of tasks (METR-2025), and matched control-group interviews with observed tasks (UKDBT-2025 §Methodology). For a team lead, the practical form is: record the last two to three months of actual volume, cycle time, rework and cost before switching anything on.

**2.4 Ranges, assumptions and the value of information.** Hubbard's Applied Information Economics treats every input as a calibrated range, computes which inputs are worth measuring better, and stops measuring when more certainty would not change the decision (HDR-AIE). He also argues that organizations tend to measure the wrong variables, because the most uncertain variables tend to be ignored (HDR-AIE, "Measure What Matters"). For AI business cases the practical consequence is: show low, base and high cases, name the two or three assumptions that drive the result (usually adoption, share of time actually redeployed, and review/rework rate), and test those first in the pilot.

**2.5 Task-level gains vs process-level gains.** Acemoglu's task-based model says macro gains depend on the fraction of tasks affected and the average task-level cost saving (NBER-ACEMOGLU-2024, Abstract). Anthropic makes the same point at task level: where AI speeds some tasks up, "others may become bottlenecks" (ANTHROPIC-2025, "As AI accelerates some tasks, others may become bottlenecks"). For a process owner: a 50% speedup on a step that is 10% of end-to-end elapsed time is a 5% effect at best, and only if the downstream step can absorb the output `(synthesis, applying the cited logic)`.

**2.6 Total cost of AI.** The FinOps Foundation lists layers beyond inference tokens: cloud compute and storage, data-centre infrastructure for self-hosted work, networking, SaaS-embedded AI priced per seat or outcome, engineering/MLOps/governance (including evaluation pipelines), data licensing, shadow AI and training costs (FINOPS-2026, "The Cost Stack Around the Token"). It also says token consumption is non-linear with user-facing activity and that unit prices falling does not mean spend falling, because modality, agent autonomy and reasoning length expand. Add the human cost the sources keep surfacing: review and rework time (WORKDAY-2026), training and change effort (UKDBT-2025 Exec Summary), and licence cost against actual use.

**2.7 Portfolio shape: quick wins, foundations, big bets.** BCG reports that 70% of potential AI value sits in core business functions and recommends focusing end-to-end on a few important workflows rather than "automating processes here and there" (BCG-2025, "Value from the Core"). Gartner's finance portfolio advice is to rebalance away from productivity-only toward decision quality and new value, and to build reusable assets such as data (GARTNER-CFO2-2026). Microsoft's adoption framework classifies use cases as "individual work" versus "business automation" and ranks them on strategic value and feasibility against a maturity level (MSFT-CAF-2026, "Prioritize AI use cases"). In plain terms: quick wins prove value and build confidence, foundations (data, evaluation, platform, skills) make later wins cheaper, and big bets change the work or the offer and need a different standard of proof.

**2.8 Measurement lag and the J-curve.** General-purpose technologies need complementary investments (processes, skills, business models) that are often intangible and poorly measured; measured productivity can dip before it rises (NBER-JCURVE-2020, Abstract). The Danish study finds that "technological change reshapes work well before it surfaces in earnings or hours" (NBER-HUMLUM-2025, Abstract). Wharton's 2025 survey reports four of five leaders expecting AI investments to pay off in about two to three years (WHARTON-2025). A business case should state the measurement horizon and the leading indicators to be used before financial outcomes show.

**2.9 Benefit realization is a management job.** Benefits do not appear because a tool is deployed. Gartner tells CFOs to "measure AI portfolio progress by realized value rather than deployment volume" (GARTNER-CFO1-2026), Wharton's 2026 findings associate strongly positive ROI with dedicated AI ownership and visible spend (WHARTON-2026, Key Takeaways), and the UK public-sector playbook points to the Treasury's five-part business case for investments approaching £10 million and agile business cases below that threshold (UKGDS-PLAYBOOK, "AI business cases").

## 3. Expert moves

Format: move, when to use it, what it reveals, source ref.

1. **Ask "who owns the freed hours and what is the next unit of work they will do?" before putting any value on time saved.** When sizing a productivity use case. Reveals whether hours convert to a destination or are absorbed. Refs: GARTNER-HR-2025 (7% provide guidelines on using time saved); WORKDAY-2026 (organizations are more likely to put AI savings back into technology, 39%, than employee development, 30%; many "simply increase workload", 32%).
2. **Split every hours-saved claim into three buckets: absorbed, redeployed, cashable.** When building the benefit line. Reveals how much of the headline is real money versus capacity. Only the cashable bucket belongs in a cost case; redeployed hours need a named output metric. `(synthesis)` Supporting logic: GARTNER-CSO-2026 ("translating time saved directly into cost savings is not always meaningful" and recommends linking gains to previously identified productivity gaps).
3. **Size on net time: subtract review, correction and added tasks.** When reading any vendor or pilot time-saving figure. Reveals gross-versus-net gaps. Refs: WORKDAY-2026 (nearly 40% of time savings lost to rework; 77% of daily users review AI work as carefully as human work); UKDBT-2025 (scheduling and image tasks took longer with Copilot, caused by poor output or added workload).
4. **Ask for last month's actual volume, cycle time and rework rate, not a typical week, before any pilot starts.** When baselining. Reveals variation and hidden rework and gives the "before" for isolation. Refs: GARTNER-CSO-2026 ("reliable baseline" for seller productivity and sales capacity); ROIINST-2014.
5. **Build in a comparison group or staggered start, even a rough one.** When designing a pilot. Reveals what changed because of AI versus because of everything else. Refs: NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023 (staggered introduction across 5,179 agents); UKDBT-2025 (control-group interviews and observed tasks); ROIINST-2014 (isolating effects is "critical").
6. **Do not rely on self-reported time alone; sample observed tasks or timestamps.** When a user says "this saves us hours". Reveals perception gaps. Refs: METR-2025 (expected +24% speed, believed +20% after a measured 19% slowdown); UKDBT-2025 (observed tasks added to address self-report bias).
7. **Express every uncertain input as a range and name the two or three that drive the answer.** When drafting the case. Reveals the assumptions to test first, typically adoption, redeployment share and rework rate `(synthesis)`. Refs: HDR-AIE ("Model What We Know Now"; "Measure What Matters").
8. **Compute which assumption is worth measuring before measuring anything.** When a team wants to "measure everything". Reveals the few variables whose uncertainty could flip the go/no-go, and avoids vanity metrics. Refs: HDR-AIE; ITANA-2019 (search-only summary: most variables in his models had zero information value).
9. **Weight each step's speedup by its share of end-to-end elapsed time and check the downstream constraint.** When mapping turns into sizing. Reveals bottlenecks that cap the process-level benefit. Refs: NBER-ACEMOGLU-2024 (fraction of tasks times average savings); ANTHROPIC-2025 (bottleneck point).
10. **Segment the benefit by user type, not an average.** When sizing a rollout. Reveals that gains concentrate in some groups. Ref: NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023 (14% average productivity gain, 34% for novice and low-skill workers, minimal for experienced workers, in customer support).
11. **Classify each candidate as individual productivity, business automation, or new value, and set a different proof standard for each.** When prioritizing. Reveals mixed portfolios where a productivity case is being judged like a growth case. Refs: MSFT-CAF-2026 (individual work vs business automation); GARTNER-CFO2-2026 (Upend initiatives more than twice as likely to report high realized value; productivity benefit plateaus unless it changes a broader decision).
12. **Score on value and feasibility with explicit gates for data readiness, skills and maturity before ranking.** When prioritizing. Reveals use cases that rank high on value but are infeasible now and belong in a "foundations" list. Refs: MSFT-CAF-2026 (score on business impact, technical complexity, resource requirements and alignment; assess maturity level and data readiness); IBM-2025 (68% of CEOs call integrated enterprise-wide data architecture critical; 50% report disconnected technology from rapid investment).
13. **Concentrate on a few end-to-end workflows instead of many scattered automations.** When the long list is more than a handful of items. Reveals diffuse effort with no coordinated value. Ref: BCG-2025 ("Value from the Core"; "Prioritization accelerates implementation": 62% of future-built initiatives deployed versus 12% for laggards).
14. **Look for external spend that AI replaces (BPO, agencies, outsourced checks) as the first cashable line.** When the user asks "where is the money". Reveals tangible savings that do not depend on headcount decisions. Ref: MIT-NANDA-2025 §6.3 (interview-based; BPO elimination of $2–10M annually in customer service and document processing, 30% decrease in external creative and content spend; self-reported and anonymized).
15. **Pair every efficiency metric with a quality guardrail and, where possible, a growth metric.** When designing KPIs. Reveals efficiency gained by quality lost, and whether the work is being redirected to value. Refs: WHARTON-2026 (35% vs 13%); WORKDAY-2026 (only 14% of employees consistently get clear, positive net outcomes).
16. **Report realized versus expected value, and treat deployment counts (pilots, licences, use cases live) as activity, not impact.** When setting up tracking. Reveals the activity-for-impact substitution. Ref: GARTNER-CFO1-2026.
17. **Build the cost stack as a checklist and ask who owns each line.** When writing the cost side. Reveals omitted layers (evaluation, governance, data licensing, SaaS-embedded AI, shadow AI). Ref: FINOPS-2026 ("The Cost Stack Around the Token").
18. **Forecast consumption, not just unit price, and state the price assumption.** When a case runs more than 12 months or involves agents. Reveals exposure to growing token volume per task; the FinOps piece says reasoning and agentic workloads consume five to thirty times more tokens per task than chat and that enterprise spend is rising while unit prices fall at fixed capability. Refs: FINOPS-2026; EPOCH-2025.
19. **Ask whether cost visibility exists at the level of the use case.** When moving from pilot to scale. Reveals whether the benefit-cost ratio can be tracked at all; KPMG reports 26% have full real-time visibility of AI operating cost. Ref: KPMG-2026.
20. **Risk-adjust the benefits and show the option value separately.** When building a financial case for a finance audience. Reveals how much of the case rests on unproven benefit, and credits future options without counting them as base benefit. Ref: FORRESTER-TEI (four components: cost, benefits, flexibility, risk); note the caveat that TEI studies are commissioned.
21. **Use full-cost ROI with isolation, and report intangibles separately instead of converting everything to money.** When a CFO asks for "the ROI". Reveals which benefits were converted, how they were isolated and what remains qualitative. Ref: ROIINST-2014 (Level 5 compares monetary benefits with fully loaded costs; intangible benefits identified separately).
22. **Choose the business-case format by scale.** When the investment is large or public-sector. Reveals whether a five-part case or an agile case is expected. Ref: UKGDS-PLAYBOOK ("AI business cases": five-part case required where investment approaches about £10 million; agile business case guidance below that; assurance for spend above £100,000 public-facing or £1 million otherwise).
23. **Define go/no-go criteria for experiments before they start.** When running pilots on more disruptive use cases. Reveals whether the team can stop a failing pilot; Gartner links abandonment after proof of concept to poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating cost or unclear business value. Refs: GARTNER-CSO-2026 ("clear go or no-go criteria for experimentation"); GARTNER-POC-2024.
24. **Name a single accountable owner and ask which approved platform or tool policy applies.** When moving from pilots to scale. Reveals fragmented ownership; Wharton's 2026 data associate dedicated AI ownership and a standardized platform with higher strongly-positive ROI (association, self-reported). Ref: WHARTON-2026 (58% vs 8% with and without a designated chief AI officer; 50% vs 9% on a single approved platform vs still experimenting).
25. **State the measurement horizon and the leading indicators up front.** When setting expectations with sponsors. Reveals a J-curve risk: early flat or negative financials with real capability building. Refs: NBER-JCURVE-2020; WHARTON-2025 (four of five expect payoff in about two to three years); MIT-NANDA-2025 §8.2 (six-month observation window noted as possibly too short for complex deployments).
26. **Read how a headline statistic was defined before quoting it.** When a sponsor quotes "95% of pilots fail". Reveals the definition and sample. In MIT NANDA the 5% refers to task-specific or custom enterprise tools whose users or executives reported marked, sustained productivity and/or P&L impact, from interviews described by the authors as directional. Ref: MIT-NANDA-2025 §3.2 and §8.2.
27. **Ask who paid for the study.** When a user brings an ROI number from a vendor. Reveals commissioned framing; Forrester says TEI studies are clearly marked "commissioned". Refs: FORRESTER-TEI; WORKDAY-2026 (vendor-published survey).
28. **Check where reinvestment is going before promising headcount savings.** When a sponsor expects labour cost reduction. Reveals that most surveyed leaders report reinvestment, not reduction. Refs: EY-2025 (17% report reduced headcount among those with gains); MIT-NANDA-2025 §6.3 (savings from reduced external spend, without material workforce reduction, in the cases described).

## 4. Question bank

Scope tags: **[T]** team, **[U]** unit, **[O]** organization. Stage tags: **[early]** (idea or first pilot), **[pilot]** (pilot in flight), **[scale]** (moving to production or rolled out).

### Phase: prioritize

- What decision will this ranking drive, and what would make you change your mind? [T/U/O] [early] (HDR-AIE: define the decision)
- Which of these ideas improve capacity, speed, quality, growth, cost, risk or experience, and what is the one metric you would use for each? [T/U] [early]
- For each idea, is it individual productivity, business automation, or new value? [U/O] [early] (MSFT-CAF-2026; GARTNER-CFO2-2026)
- What is the last month's actual volume, cycle time, error and rework rate for this step? [T] [early] (move 4)
- What share of the end-to-end elapsed time does this step represent, and what constrains the next step? [T/U] [early] (move 9)
- What data would the use case need, and where is it, in what quality, and who can grant access? [T/U] [early] (MSFT-CAF-2026 data inventory)
- Which of the ideas depend on a foundation that does not exist yet (data, evaluation set, platform, skills)? [U/O] [early] (MSFT-CAF-2026; IBM-2025)
- Where do we spend money today on external providers (BPO, agencies, outsourced reviews) that the work could replace? [U/O] [early] (MIT-NANDA-2025 §6.3)
- Which two or three assumptions, if wrong, would change the ranking? [T/U/O] [early] (HDR-AIE)
- How many workflows are we trying to change at once, and could we go end-to-end on fewer? [U/O] [early] (BCG-2025)
- Who would use this tomorrow, and are they novices, experts or a mix? [T/U] [early] (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023)
- What is the risk if the AI is wrong here, and who reviews? [T/U/O] [early] (feeds the risk lever; DBT report notes hallucinations and inconsistent quality assurance, UKDBT-2025 Exec Summary)
- For a pilot already running: which results are measured and which are reported by users? [T/U] [pilot] (METR-2025; UKDBT-2025)
- For the portfolio: what share of ideas is productivity-only versus decision-quality or new-value? [U/O] [scale] (GARTNER-CFO2-2026)

### Phase: business-case

- What are we comparing against: current process, a control group, or an estimate? [T/U/O] [pilot] (ROIINST-2014)
- Of the hours saved, how many will be absorbed, redeployed to a named output, or removed as cost? [T/U] [pilot/scale] (moves 1–2)
- What net time remains after review, correction and new tasks the tool creates? [T/U] [pilot] (WORKDAY-2026; UKDBT-2025)
- What is the low, base and high case, and what are the assumptions that separate them? [T/U/O] [early/pilot] (HDR-AIE)
- Which benefit lines are cashable, and who in finance signs off the baseline? [U/O] [pilot/scale] (GARTNER-CSO-2026 on CFO support)
- What does the cost stack look like: licences, tokens or consumption, platform, integration, evaluation, governance, training, change, support? [U/O] [early/pilot] (FINOPS-2026)
- What is the expected consumption in year two if usage and agent autonomy grow? [U/O] [scale] (FINOPS-2026)
- Which benefits are outcome improvements (quality, speed to client, win rate, retention) rather than time? [T/U/O] [early/pilot] (WHARTON-2026; GARTNER-CSO-2026)
- What is the measured or assumed quality effect, and how would we notice if it went the wrong way? [T/U] [pilot] (WORKDAY-2026)
- Which adjacent initiatives claim the same benefit? `(synthesis)` [U/O] [early]
- What would a CFO need to see to treat this as a base case rather than an upside? [U/O] [early/pilot] (FORRESTER-TEI; GARTNER-CFO1-2026)
- What business-case format does our organization require at this spend level? [U/O] [early] (UKGDS-PLAYBOOK)
- What are the options this unlocks, valued separately from the base benefit? [U/O] [early] (FORRESTER-TEI)

### Phase: plan-and-control

- Who is the single accountable owner for the benefit, and who owns the data for tracking it? [T/U/O] [scale] (WHARTON-2026)
- Which three to five KPIs will we track, which are leading versus lagging, and what is the quality guardrail? [T/U/O] [pilot/scale] (BCG-2025: more than 60% of future-built firms rigorously track AI value)
- What will we do with the freed time, and what is the communication to the team? [T/U] [scale] (GARTNER-HR-2025; WORKDAY-2026)
- How will cost per use case or per outcome be visible monthly? [U/O] [scale] (KPMG-2026; FINOPS-2026)
- What are the go/no-go criteria and review dates for the pilot and for the first scale step? [T/U/O] [pilot] (GARTNER-CSO-2026)
- Which roles, procedures and controls need to change for the benefit to appear? [T/U/O] [scale] (WORKDAY-2026: in most organizations fewer than half of roles updated; WHARTON-2026)
- How will we re-baseline when the model, vendor pricing or process changes? [U/O] [scale] (FINOPS-2026; METR-2026 on measurement drift)
- When do we expect financial signal, and what do we report until then? [U/O] [scale] (NBER-JCURVE-2020; WHARTON-2025)
- How do we report across units so benefits are not double counted at roll-up? `(synthesis)` [O] [scale]
- Who reviews realized versus expected value, and how often? [U/O] [scale] (GARTNER-CFO1-2026)

## 5. Common traps

| Trap | Why it happens | Evidence | How to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hours x hourly rate = savings.** | Time saved is easy to count and looks like money. | UK DBT: no evidence that time savings led to improved productivity (UKDBT-2025). UK cross-government: could not identify how saved time was spent (UKGDS-2025 §6). Gartner: translating time saved directly into cost savings is not always meaningful (GARTNER-CSO-2026). | Three-bucket split (move 2); name the destination and owner. |
| **Trusting self-reports.** | Users sincerely believe they are faster. | METR randomized trial: expected +24% speedup, measured 19% longer, believed 20% faster afterward (METR-2025). DBT diary study had a 32% response rate (UKDBT-2025 Exec Summary). | Sample observed tasks or system data; report self-report as such. |
| **Ignoring rework and verification.** | Cost of checking sits with the user, outside the business case. | Workday: nearly 40% of time savings lost to rework (WORKDAY-2026). HBR/BetterUp: 40% of 1,150 US employees surveyed report receiving low-quality AI-generated "workslop" in the last month; those affected report an average of 1 hour 56 minutes dealing with each instance (HBR-NIEDERHOFFER-2025). | Size on net time; add a quality KPI and a review-time line. |
| **Using an average across heterogeneous users and tasks.** | One pilot number is extrapolated to everyone. | Customer-support study: 14% average, 34% for novices, minimal for experienced workers (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023). DBT: some tasks saved time, others took longer (UKDBT-2025). | Segment by user and task type. |
| **Counting activity as impact.** | Licences, pilots and use cases live are easy to report. | Gartner to CFOs: "must not mistake activity for impact" (GARTNER-CFO1-2026). | Report realized versus expected value. |
| **Efficiency-only portfolio.** | Productivity cases are easier to justify. | Gartner finance survey: 45% of AI investments lean to productivity versus 20% to decision quality; the benefit plateaus unless efficiency changes a broader decision (GARTNER-CFO2-2026). Wharton: 35% vs 13% strongly positive ROI with a growth measure vs efficiency alone (association; self-reported) (WHARTON-2026). | Add growth, quality and decision metrics; classify use-case type (move 11). |
| **Pilot-to-scale extrapolation.** | Pilots use enthusiastic volunteers and bounded scope. | DBT tested whether results differed for users who volunteered versus those who did not (UKDBT-2025 Table 2), a reminder that volunteer pilots can flatter results. MIT NANDA: about 5% of task-specific tools reached production (self-reported interviews) (MIT-NANDA-2025 §3.2). Gartner predicts at least 30% of GenAI projects abandoned after proof of concept by end of 2025 (prediction) (GARTNER-POC-2024). | Pilot design with comparison group; scale-up cost and adoption assumptions as ranges. |
| **Taking a headline failure rate at face value.** | A viral statistic hides the definition. | MIT NANDA's "95%" covers a specific definition and a sample the authors call directional (MIT-NANDA-2025 §3.2, §8.2). | Quote what it measures and who measured it (move 26). |
| **Costing only licences or tokens.** | Those are the visible lines. | FinOps layers list (FINOPS-2026); KPMG 26% real-time cost visibility (KPMG-2026); Gartner cost range of $5M to $20M for GenAI deployment approaches including business-model innovation (GARTNER-POC-2024). | Cost-stack checklist and consumption forecast (moves 17–19). |

## 7. Worked examples

### 7.1 Sizing a drafting-and-review assistant for a professional-services team (illustrative; advisor method, not a case study)

Scenario: a 40-person advisory team where a first-draft report step is the target. `(synthesis)` of cited methods; numbers are placeholders to show the arithmetic, not evidence.

1. **Baseline** (move 4): last three months of actual draft hours per report, reviewer rework hours, and client-ready turnaround time, taken from time records.
2. **Gross time saved as a range**, not a point: for example low 5%, base 15%, high 25% of drafting hours, with the range anchored to observed tasks from a 4-week pilot with a matched comparison group (moves 5–7). The pilot should be read against the published pattern that average gains hide segment differences (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023) and that some tasks take longer (UKDBT-2025).
3. **Net out review and rework** (move 3). If reviewers spend extra time on first drafts, subtract it. WORKDAY-2026 reports nearly 40% of time savings lost to rework in its survey, which is a reason to treat the net share as an assumption to measure, not to assume.
4. **Weight by the step's share of the end-to-end time** (move 9). If drafting is a fixed share of delivery time and client review is the constraint, the process-level benefit is capped by that share.
5. **Allocate net hours to destinations** (moves 1–2): absorbed, redeployed (for example more reports per quarter, or more time on client advice), cashable (less contractor drafting). Only cashable hours enter the cost line. Redeployed hours enter as an output KPI with an owner (reports completed per quarter, win rate, utilization) so the benefit appears in a business measure and not only in time.
6. **Cost stack** (move 17): licences, consumption, integration, evaluation of output quality, training, review time, governance.
7. **Present low/base/high with the two or three assumptions** (move 7) and the go/no-go criteria at 90 days (move 23).

### 7.2 UK Department for Business and Trade Copilot pilot: what a careful evaluation says and does not say

Source: UKDBT-2025. A government department ran a three-month pilot (Oct–Dec 2024, 1,000 licences) and evaluated it through diaries, interviews with participants and non-user control colleagues, and observed tasks. Findings: high satisfaction among many users, small time savings across most use cases with written tasks showing the most, extra time on scheduling and image tasks, and no evidence that time savings had led to improved productivity; the evaluation recommends further work on value for money. Lessons for the advisor: (a) a satisfied user base is not a benefit; (b) the method (control interviews, observed tasks) is the part to copy; (c) the authors themselves describe the design as lacking a counterfactual, so even this is bounded evidence. The same period's cross-government experiment (UKGDS-2025) reported an average of 26 minutes saved per day but could not tell how the time was used, which is the benefit-realization gap in one sentence.

### 7.3 Customer-support assistant: measuring a distribution, not an average

Source: NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023. Data on 5,179 customer-support agents with a staggered rollout of a generative AI assistant. Productivity (issues resolved per hour) rose 14% on average, 34% for novice and low-skill workers and minimally for experienced workers; the authors also report improved customer sentiment and employee retention. Lessons: (a) the rollout design gives a counterfactual; (b) benefits come in more than one lever (productivity, customer experience, retention), so the case should show each separately; (c) a unit with a different skill mix will not replicate the average. Caveat for the advisor: this is a customer-support setting, an adjacent process to most professional-services work, so the finding caps at moderate confidence when applied to advisory, legal or audit work.

### 7.4 A finance function rebalancing its AI portfolio

Source: GARTNER-CFO1-2026, GARTNER-CFO2-2026. Gartner's March 2026 survey of 204 finance leaders found 66% of AI-adopting finance organizations citing efficiency and productivity as a top benefit, while 63% said implementation was slower than expected and the weakest-rated areas were implementation speed and analytics impact. Investments leaned 45% to productivity versus 20% to decision quality. Gartner's advice: measure realized value instead of deployment volume, rebalance toward decision quality and new value, take a portfolio approach and build reusable assets such as data. Lesson: for a unit or practice head, a prioritization pass should classify the long list by value mode and ask what share of the portfolio is aimed at decisions and outcomes the board cares about, not only at task time.

## 8. Mapping to phases

### Prioritize

- Contributes the value-lever vocabulary (capacity, speed, quality, growth, cost, risk, experience) and the idea that each lever needs its own metric and proof standard.
- Supplies a scoring approach: value (impact on a named lever and metric) against feasibility (data readiness, skills and maturity, integration, risk), with explicit gates before ranking (MSFT-CAF-2026), plus use-case classification (individual work, business automation, new value; GARTNER-CFO2-2026).
- Supplies portfolio logic: quick wins to prove value and build confidence, foundations that make later wins cheaper, big bets with different proof standards; concentrate on a few end-to-end workflows (BCG-2025).
- Supplies screening questions: step share of end-to-end time, who uses it (novice or expert), external spend that could be displaced, and which assumptions would change the ranking (value of information).
- Supplies cautions: do not rank on hours-saved alone; do not quote a headline failure statistic without its definition.

### Business-case

- Contributes the benefit logic: hours released are converted to absorbed, redeployed or cashable, with only cashable hours entering a cost case; other benefits stated as outcome metrics.
- Contributes the baseline and isolation requirement and the three practical designs seen in the sources (comparison group, staggered rollout, observed tasks).
- Contributes range-based sizing with named assumptions, risk adjustment and separate option value (HDR-AIE; FORRESTER-TEI), and the format question (five-part vs agile case at scale; UKGDS-PLAYBOOK).
- Contributes the cost stack and consumption forecast (FINOPS-2026) plus human costs (review, training, change).
- Contributes honest evidence framing: what independent studies show, how much is self-reported, and where the sample or setting limits the conclusion.

### Plan-and-control

- Contributes KPI design: three to five KPIs, leading and lagging, a quality guardrail and a cost-per-outcome view; report realized versus expected value (GARTNER-CFO1-2026).
- Contributes benefit-realization mechanics: a named owner for each benefit, a destination for freed time, role and process changes, go/no-go gates, review cadence and re-baselining when models, prices or processes change.
- Contributes cost control: use-case-level cost visibility (KPMG-2026), consumption monitoring and a refresh of price assumptions (FINOPS-2026; EPOCH-2025).
- Contributes expectation setting: measurement horizon, J-curve and leading indicators (NBER-JCURVE-2020; WHARTON-2025).
- At higher levels (practice leaders, heads of AI), contributes roll-up discipline: consistent value definitions across units, a view of overlap between initiatives, and a portfolio shape reviewed against growth and decision-quality outcomes (GARTNER-CFO2-2026; BCG-2025). The double-counting check at roll-up is a `(synthesis)` and no source in this pass tests it.

## 10. Gaps

- **No independent measurement in professional-services work.** Independent studies here cover customer support (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023), software development (METR), UK civil-service work (UKGDS, UKDBT, self-reported or weakly counterfactual) and Danish labour records (NBER-HUMLUM-2025). I found no independent, published measurement of realized value (as opposed to time saved) for legal, audit, consulting or advisory work. The one professional-services datapoint set (Wolters Kluwer legal) is search-only and vendor-published.
- **Primary documents not reached:** McKinsey State of AI 2025, PwC 29th Global CEO Survey, Gartner's May 2026 "19% no time saved" release, Wharton's full 2026 and 2025 reports (only the web summaries were read, so sample sizes and exact metric percentages were not confirmed), Workday's full report, Forrester's survey base for the 15% EBITDA figure, KPMG's sample size, and the Itana summary of Hubbard. Their figures are marked or excluded accordingly.
- **Books not read directly.** Hubbard's *How to Measure Anything*, Phillips's ROI Methodology books and Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb's *Prediction Machines* were represented only through author-firm pages, institute papers or summaries. The HBR article by Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb returned only a paywalled stub, so no claim here rests on it. The Hubbard "measurement inversion" (the claim that the most valuable variables are those least measured) is the author's own account of his client work and is not independently verified.
- **Verified numbers not found:** the BCG-Harvard "jagged frontier" field experiment (quality and speed effects for consultants, and the reduced accuracy on tasks outside the frontier) is directly relevant to the quality lever for knowledge work, but the paper returned 403 and search results did not confirm figures, so none are reported.
- **Scoring frameworks beyond vendor guidance.** The prioritization method here rests on Microsoft's adoption framework, BCG's portfolio findings and Gartner's use-case classification. I did not find an independent evaluation of any scoring framework's predictive validity. Any "impact vs feasibility" matrix should be presented as a facilitation device, not a validated model.
- **Quick wins vs foundations vs big bets.** The three-way language is a practitioner convention. The sources support the underlying ideas (productivity cases plateau, new-value cases report higher realized value, foundations such as data and platforms matter) but none defines this exact taxonomy with evidence on mix.
- **Total cost figures are mostly commentary.** Gartner's $5M–$20M range is from a 2024 release and covers approaches such as business-model innovation, not a typical team-level deployment. The FinOps article's 5–30x token multiplier and AT&T and Google volume figures are cited by its author from other parties and were not checked at source. No source read gives a typical cost split between inference, platform, evaluation, change and support for a knowledge-work use case.
- **Benefit realization practice.** I found no primary practitioner guidance on benefits-realization management specific to AI (for example from a project-management body); the plan-and-control content rests on Gartner, Wharton, BCG and UK government sources. The UK Green Book and five-case guidance were represented only by the landing page and the AI Playbook's summary.
- **Date gaps.** Forrester predictions page, Epoch data insight, Deloitte report page, UK AI Playbook and Hubbard pages did not show a publication date; each is flagged in section 9.
- **Currency.** The Wharton 2026 report was published three days before this research date and has had no external scrutiny. METR's 2026 follow-up declares its own data unreliable, so no current independent speedup estimate for software development is available here.
