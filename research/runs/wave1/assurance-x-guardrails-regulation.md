# Assurance x guardrails-regulation: the regulatory and professional landscape for AI in assurance

- **Archetype:** assurance. External audit and other assurance services (financial statement and ICFR audits, SOC, sustainability and other attest), performed by firms of all sizes; overlapping in-house internal audit.
- **Topic:** guardrails-regulation. A consolidated map a leader can use.
- **Prepared:** 2026-10-16. Source landscape: `research/runs/wave1/landscape-assurance.md`.
- **Method note.** Pages and PDFs were fetched with `curl` (and the `r.jina.ai` mirror for pcaobus.org pages) and read as text. Two web searches were used, both on liability and insurance. Source keys follow `PUBLISHER-SHORT-YYYY` and reuse the landscape's keys where one exists. "Locator" means a printed page, section or paragraph. Quotations are under 25 words. Dates are real publication dates where the page gave one.
- **Reading rule.** "Guidance" in this file is non-binding unless stated. The binding requirements are ISQM/ISA (and national equivalents), PCAOB standards, the AICPA Code and the law. Several regulators say outright that their AI guidance creates no new requirement (FRC-GENAI-2026; DAC-AUDITAI-2026; NFRA-CAALLEY-2026).

---

## 1. Summary

- **No regulator prohibits AI in audit, and every one that has spoken keeps the human auditor accountable.** The FRC says regulatory accountability is "unchanged" and the human auditor is always accountable (FRC-NEWS-2026, guidance page). IFIAR asks firms to make sure auditors are in control and keep responsibility for judgements (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.4). NFRA (India) says the audit opinion belongs to an identified auditor, not to a tool (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026, "Auditor responsibility remains non-delegable"). The AICPA says the auditor "remains responsible for the audit and the audit opinion" (JOFA-AIPA-2026).
- **The binding rules have not changed much yet; the guidance layer has.** The FRC issued the first regulator guidance on generative and agentic AI on 30 March 2026 (FRC-NEWS-2026). The AICPA issued its first auditor AI practice aid on 13 October 2026 (AICPA-PA-2026). The IAASB's exposure drafts on ISA 330/500/520 propose no technology requirement and chose guidance, because it judged a technology requirement premature (IAASB-ED500-2026, EM paras 66-69, p.20). Binding changes with dates are mostly not AI-specific: PCAOB QC 1000 amendments effective 15 Dec 2026 (pending SEC approval), PCAOB technology-assisted-analysis amendments effective for fiscal years beginning on or after 15 Dec 2025, AICPA SAS 150 and 151 effective for periods ending on or after 15 Dec 2028.
- **Regulators' inspection lens is moving from the single tool to the firm's system of quality management.** The FRC's new supervision model puts the system of quality management "at the heart" of supervision, starting April 2026 for the largest firms (FRC-SUPERVISION-2026). IFIAR frames tool oversight under ISQM 1 and warns that monitoring may be fragmented between networks and member firms (IFIAR-TTF-2026, pp.6, 8). The PCAOB says it will consider a quality-control-focused approach to inspections (JOFA-QC1000-2026). The AICPA peer review board has done the same for SOC 2 firms that lean on tool platforms (JOFA-PEER-2026).
- **There is now a first public inspection finding on AI-type tools.** CPAB (Canada) reported in Dec 2025 that in 2025 inspections some auditors used automated tools as the primary evidence for a class of transactions, with limited validation of input completeness and accuracy, and in one tool no further testing of items the algorithm rated low risk (CPAB-EVOLVING-2025, p.1). The landscape said none had been found. It is a single regulator's finding, and the source does not name the tool type beyond "ATTs".
- **Documentation expectations are specific, and proportionate.** The FRC splits documentation between central records (what the tool is, how it was developed, why the firm trusts it, training, explainability) and the audit file (version, configuration and prompts, why use was appropriate, approval, how outputs were used). The more widely used the tool, the more sits centrally (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, pp.13-16). NFRA wants a file an unconnected experienced auditor can follow to see which tool processed which balance and how much human review followed (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026, "Audit documentation and training to change").
- **Client data confidentiality is the least-standardised area.** Most regulator material says only that firms stay responsible (NFRA, IRBA, AUASB, IESBA). The AICPA ethics staff give the most practical test: before pasting client data into an AI system, ask whether it is confidential client information and whether specific consent is needed (JOFA-ETHAI-2026). The EU data-protection layer (GDPR, EDPB Opinion 28/2024) is general, not audit-specific, and I found no audit-regulator document that works through GDPR for audit tools.
- **The EU AI Act mostly reaches audit firms as deployers of general-purpose tools and as employers, not as providers of high-risk systems.** The high-risk obligations were delayed by the AI Omnibus to 2 Dec 2027 (stand-alone Annex III systems) and 2 Aug 2028 (embedded in products); the Omnibus was formally adopted by Parliament and Council by 1 Jul 2026 (ADDLESHAW-AIOMNIBUS-2026). Annex III as published names employment and credit scoring of natural persons but, as I read it, no category for audit or assurance services (AIACT-ANNEX3, items 4 and 5). The AI literacy duty (Article 4) has applied since 2 Feb 2025, softened by the Omnibus (GIBSONDUNN-AIOMNIBUS-2026, section 2). Whether any given audit tool is "high-risk" is a use-by-use question I could not resolve from public sources.
- **Liability: no reported case turns on AI use in an audit, and cover is unsettled.** Law-firm commentary expects ordinary negligence principles to apply and says an AI error is unlikely by itself to be a defence (DAC-AUDITAI-2026). Insurance trade press says standard E&O generally covers AI-related negligence unless a policy has an AI exclusion, while some carriers are adding exclusions in other professional lines (BUSINESSINS-AIEO-2026). I found nothing specific to audit-firm policies.

Observations.
- Technology is mostly embedded in non-AI standard-setting. The IAASB says the ED ISA 500 text adds application material (technology-agnostic) and pushes tool-specific direction into guidance (IAASB-ED500-2026, EM para 69). The ED also lists data protection, security and privacy laws as a factor affecting the auditor's use of technological tools, with an example that privacy or secrecy laws may prohibit use of certain sensitive client information in some tools "without proper consent or safeguards" (IAASB-ED500-2026, application material table; example under restrictions on access).
- The sequencing for a firm planning to 2027: IAASB responses (15 Dec 2026), QC 1000 and ISQM-based documentation now, PCAOB independence proposal (Q1 2027), IAASB guides (2027), AICPA SASs (2028 periods). The IAASB timetable itself says milestones "may be adjusted" (IAASB-TQMPLAN-2026, para 48).

### 2.3 Inspection focus on technology

- **FRC (UK).**
  - The thematic review of the six largest firms found most had well-established certification processes for automated tools and techniques, some less mature and without documented policies, and firms were still adapting certification for AI-specific risks and building ways to monitor usage and impact on audit quality (FRC-ATT-2025, exec summary).
  - From April 2026 the FRC's revised audit supervision puts firms' systems of quality management at the centre, supported by thematic reviews and "corroboratory inspections" (FRC-SUPERVISION-2026).
  - The FRC says its March 2026 generative AI guidance is "not a response to identified quality deficiencies" (FRC-NEWS-2026).
- **IFIAR.**
  - In April 2026 IFIAR reported that the GPPC networks collectively reported about a 30% year-over-year increase in AI-enabled tools in 2025, with a footnote that reporting practices differ across networks (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.5).
  - Members see automated tools frequently in journal-entry testing, internal-controls testing and risk assessment, with an increase in substantive procedures; percentages for 2025 are in a chart I could not read as numbers, so the 2024 figures stand: journal entries 88%, risk assessment 55%, substantive 55%, controls testing 43%, IT applications 39% "frequently" seen (IFIAR-TTF-2025, table, pp.4-5 of the printed report; IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.9 and footnotes 3-4).
  - IFIAR says responsibility for monitoring tools sits at network level in some networks and member-firm level in others, and "there is a risk" that monitoring is fragmented (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.8).
  - IFIAR also flags the audited entity's side: documents such as invoices and bank statements "may originate from AI systems" and be used as audit evidence, and auditors should be alert to deliberate or adversarial misuse of AI within audited entities (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.10).
  - IFIAR names agentic AI as something firms plan to explore and asks for early engagement with local regulators and IFIAR when technology may significantly alter the work performed (IFIAR-TTF-2026, pp.4-5).
- **CPAB (Canada).**
  - The Dec 2025 publication reports the inspection finding described in the Summary: automated tools as primary evidence, limited validation of data inputs, and no extra testing of low-risk-rated transactions, which "raised concerns" about transparency of risk classification and whether the auditor sufficiently challenged outputs (CPAB-EVOLVING-2025, p.1).
  - Its Sep 2024 publication had said AI-enabled tools were in early stages with limited implementations in inspected public-company files (CPAB-AI-2024, p.1).
- **PCAOB.**
  - Inspections director Christine Gunia said in Dec 2025 that inspectors would focus on AI and on private-equity transactions in 2026, per secondary reports (NYSSCPA-BOTIC-2025; CAALLEY-PCAOBPE-2025 via landscape).
  - Acting Chair Botic said AI has the potential to improve risk assessment and population testing but also to erode skepticism, citing research on critical thinking (NYSSCPA-BOTIC-2025; KSLAW-BOTIC-2025).
  - The Inspections Modernization Council was formed in 2026; its posted discussion materials are staff drafts not approved by the Board. The IMC page lists 12 members including Forvis Mazars, Grant Thornton, CPAB and COSO (PCAOB-IMC-PAGE-2026). Content of the materials is from secondary reports in the landscape (STARLING-IMC-2026).
  - After adoption of QC 1000 amendments the Board is positioned to "consider a quality control-focused approach to future inspections" (JOFA-QC1000-2026).
  - I found no PCAOB inspection-report finding specific to AI tools.
- **AICPA Peer Review Board (US, non-issuer firms).**
  - The May 2026 reviewer alert warns about SOC 2 engagements with identical reports, risk assessments, sample sizes and testing, which peer reviewers call "nonconforming". Reviewers are to understand the firm's use of technology, including external SOC platforms, and the reasonableness of engagement timelines. Staff outreach to team captains was planned from 1 Jun (JOFA-PEER-2026).
  - That is the clearest US case of a tool-driven business model becoming a peer review finding (SOC 2, not AI specifically; the article is about third-party SOC platforms and compliance tools).

### 2.4 Documentation and evidence expectations for AI use

**What the FRC expects (UK; the most detailed).**
- Central documentation, for AI tools of any kind used on an engagement: a conceptual description and objective; when the tool is appropriate to use; how it was developed, including data sources and why their use is lawful and permitted by professional standards; why the firm is confident it works as intended (governance, certification steps); training and guidance, including strategies to mitigate automation bias; and how the tool was designed to be explainable (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, central tables, pp.13-15).
- Third-party tools: the same expectations apply, but the firm may not get full development history and may rely on independent assurance that the tool operates as intended (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, "Considerations where the tool is obtained from a 3rd party", pp.13-15).
- Audit file, for ATTs that use AI: brief explanation and objective, tool and model version, configuration and "what data or prompts" were input, the team's assessment against central criteria (including how it ensured input data was complete and accurate), evidence of central approval, and how outputs were used to conclude or inform further procedures (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, audit-file table, p.16).
- Proportionality is explicit: the guidance "is not prescriptive" and the requirements remain only those in ISQMs and ISAs (UK); for a language-model tool whose outputs are all checked back to cited sources, "it may well be appropriate to document less". The FRC warns that over-documentation can divert resources (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, pp.6-7).
- For generative and agentic tools, the FRC examples show the engagement team documenting on the file that an AI-enabled tool was used, and how its output informed further work and conclusions, with the rest retained centrally (FRC-GENAI-2026, illustrative example 1, use-case page).

**What other regulators say.**
- IFIAR (Apr 2026): firms should "document guidance on appropriate tool usage, including limitations and risk considerations", run initial validation and approval protocols for all tools, and re-approve when tools or underlying models are updated (IFIAR-TTF-2026, pp.6-7). Engagement leaders should set out at the start the planned uses of AI, and senior team members should reflect on the overall use of technology in the file and whether skepticism was maintained (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.7).
- CPAB (Dec 2025): keep "a clear, documented roadmap" of steps taken or decisions made by tools, so that an experienced auditor with no prior involvement can understand nature, timing and extent, results, and significant judgments (CPAB-EVOLVING-2025, p.5).
- NFRA (Jul 2026): documentation proportionate to the materiality of the output to the opinion; documentation should show which tool processed which balance or assertion and the extent of human review; material model, data-source or vendor changes should trigger revalidation (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026).
- NFRA and the PCAOB hold the line on evidence: NFRA says there is "no separate, lower evidentiary bar for technology generated information", and that auditors should not accept a tool's low-risk classification without periodically testing it (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026, "Professional scepticism cannot be delegated to technology"). The PCAOB's TAA amendments are designed to reduce the likelihood that an auditor issues a report without sufficient appropriate evidence when using technology-assisted analysis (PCAOB-TAA-2024, "Why Did the PCAOB Adopt These Amendments?").
- FRC position on persuasiveness: AI-enabled procedures need not match the persuasiveness of the procedure replaced, provided the methodology does not overstate it and the approach meets the standards; for whole-population outputs, insight on each item "may be less conclusive" and professional judgement decides what conclusions are supportable (FRC-GENAI-2026, "Risk of non-compliant methodology").
- IAASB on evidence: the board considered but did not propose a requirement to assess inputs, tool operation and outputs, because for GenAI tools it may not be possible to fully evaluate inputs or confirm the tool operates as designed (IAASB-ED500-2026, EM para 68, p.20). That means a firm cannot point to a standard that sets the evidence test for an LLM-based tool; it has to build it from the existing evidence standards plus guidance.
- AICPA: the practice aid recommends planning intended AI use in engagements and says engagement partners should understand the AI tools used, ensure the firm has approved them and that staff use them correctly (AICPA-PA-2026). The Landscape adds that AU-C 210 does not require naming AI tools in the engagement letter (landscape R20; not re-checked this run). IRBA recommended in 2023 including language in engagement letters covering AI use (IRBA-AI-2024, p.1).

**Client-side evidence.**
- The AICPA practice aid's first part addresses the audited entity's AI use: the effect of data quantity, quality, accuracy and completeness on AI reliability, new risks (data, model, hallucination, explainability, bias), foundational controls over AI in financial reporting, and the audit team's capability (AICPA-PA-2026, "Considering an organization's use of AI").
- COSO's generative AI guidance (Feb 2026) organises GenAI uses into eight capability types, with minimum control expectations and metrics designed to support "audit evidence collection" (JOFA-COSO-2026).

### 2.5 Client data confidentiality and data protection

**Professional-ethics layer (binding for members).**
- IESBA Code: confidentiality extends to the full data lifecycle (collection, use, transfer, storage, dissemination, lawful destruction), and use of client data for AI model training "requires proper authorization" (IESBA-SNAPSHOT-2026, Q4, confidentiality bullet).
- AICPA Code: the Confidential Client Information Rule (ET 1.700.001) applies. Staff advise asking before pasting client data into an AI system: is it confidential client information, is consent required and given specifically, could this result in disclosure to third parties; some public GenAI tools use inputs to train future models (JOFA-ETHAI-2026, "AI can create confidentiality risks quickly").

**Regulator and standard-setter statements.**
- PCAOB staff (Jul 2024): data privacy and data security were areas of focus; some firms limited what could be uploaded to GenAI tools to stop confidential information such as data of companies under audit becoming public; some firms did not allow GenAI on audit or attest procedures for data-privacy and reliability reasons (PCAOB-GENAI-2024, p.4; staff views, not Board policy).
- AUASB (Jul 2025): firms must manage data privacy and security risk because "much of the data that auditors use is proprietary data of the audited entity" (AUASB-AI-2025, "Consideration on using AI tools in audits").
- IRBA (Sep 2024): GenAI may lead to unintended disclosure of sensitive information; confidentiality was not explicit in its 2023 communique but is now an ongoing concern (IRBA-AI-2024, p.2).
- NFRA (Jul 2026): firms remain responsible for client and personal data processed through third-party and cloud tools, under ethics rules and India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; a firm "cannot rely on a vendor's terms of service (without validation)"; vendor contracts should cover data protection, IP, explainability documentation, audit rights and continuity; attention to cross-border transfers (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026, "Data protection responsibility stays with audit firms"; "Greater scrutiny of technology vendors").
- FRC: the generative AI guidance scopes itself to audit-quality risks but lists cybersecurity risk "if audited entity data is input into cloud-based AI applications" as an out-of-scope risk dimension (FRC-GENAI-2026, p.6). The FRC AI in Audit guidance asks firms to document why use of any data to train or test a tool is permitted by law, regulation and professional standards (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, central table "How the tool was developed", p.13).
- IAASB: ED ISA 500 treats privacy and data-protection laws as a factor in using tools (see 2.2).
- IFIAR: includes monitoring emerging regulations on technology and data security as an emerging practice (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.9).

**Law.**
- EDPB Opinion 28/2024 (18 Dec 2024), on personal data in AI models: whether a model is anonymous is a case-by-case assessment; legitimate interest can be a legal basis for development or deployment only with a three-step test and strict necessity; unlawfully processed personal data in development can affect the lawfulness of deployment unless the model is anonymised (EDPB-OPINION-2024). It does not address audit.
- EU AI Act Article 26(9): deployers of high-risk systems use provider information to carry out a data protection impact assessment under GDPR where applicable (AIACT-ART26, para 9 as listed in the article's last paragraph). This applies only if a tool is high-risk (see 2.6).
- UK law-firm commentary lists "data protection impact assessments where appropriate" among governance steps for audit firms (DAC-AUDITAI-2026, "Governance: What Should Audit Firms Be Doing Now?").
- Accountancy Europe (Nov 2024) names confidentiality, data reliability and bias as key concerns and notes EU AI Act documentation duties (ACCTEU-CAQ-2024).

**What I could not find.** An audit-regulator or standard-setter document that works through GDPR roles (controller or processor, lawful basis, cross-border transfer) for an audit firm's AI tools, or addresses client consent wording in engagement letters in the EU. See Gaps.

**Practitioner controls.** AICPA's JofA piece suggests enterprise AI versions with contractual protections, data classification, least-privilege agent permissions and human approval for higher-risk agent actions (JOFA-CYBER-2026). A law-firm white paper for CPA firms advises prohibiting or tightly limiting input of confidential client information into public models, reviewing vendor terms (retention, training use, breach notification), and testing for data leakage and prompt injection (HEPLER-LIAB-2026, "Generative AI Governance and Risk Mitigation").

### 2.6 EU AI Act implications for firms and clients

- **Timeline.** The AI Act entered into force 1 Aug 2024. The Omnibus defers high-risk obligations to 2 Dec 2027 (stand-alone Annex III) and 2 Aug 2028 (embedded in Annex I products). Article 50 transparency obligations were due 2 Aug 2026, with a grace period to 2 Dec 2026 for the watermarking duty on systems already on the market. The Omnibus was formally adopted by Parliament and Council (reported 1 Jul 2026); publication in the Official Journal and entry into force "on the third day after publication" were still to follow at that date (ADDLESHAW-AIOMNIBUS-2026; GIBSONDUNN-AIOMNIBUS-2026, sections 1-3). I did not confirm Official Journal publication. The separate Digital Omnibus (which would amend GDPR, NIS2 and the Data Act) had not passed as of 1 Jul 2026 (ADDLESHAW-AIOMNIBUS-2026).
- **AI literacy.** The Article 4 duty, applicable since 2 Feb 2025, was kept but softened: providers and deployers must support staff AI literacy rather than guarantee a level (GIBSONDUNN-AIOMNIBUS-2026, section 2; ADDLESHAW-AIOMNIBUS-2026). This applies to firms as deployers of any AI system, not only high-risk ones. Whether it applies to a firm depends on EU nexus; I did not test this for non-EU firms.
- **High-risk categories.** Annex III includes AI used for recruitment and selection, and for decisions on terms of work, promotion, termination, task allocation based on individual behaviour or traits, and monitoring and evaluation of performance and behaviour; also credit scoring of natural persons and life and health insurance risk assessment and pricing (AIACT-ANNEX3, items 4 and 5). I found no item naming audit or assurance. Inference: an EU audit firm's AI staffing, scheduling or performance-evaluation tool (for example, tools like RSM's "Luca Resourcing", CAQ-RSM-2026 via landscape) could fall under item 4 depending on function and design. This is my reading, not a regulator's statement.
- **Deployer duties if high-risk.** Human oversight by people with competence, training and authority; monitoring; log retention; DPIA support (AIACT-ART26, paras 2, and following).
- **Client side.** Clients using AI for credit scoring of natural persons or HR decisions may be in scope from Dec 2027, which creates audit-relevant compliance exposure (inference; the Annex III list is from AIACT-ANNEX3). The AICPA practice aid treats an entity's AI use in financial reporting as an acceptance, risk-assessment and controls matter regardless of jurisdiction (AICPA-PA-2026).
- **Assurance over AI as a service line.** KPMG International achieved ISO/IEC 42001 certification (announced Dec 2025; certification by Schellman) and KPMG LLP achieved AIUC-1 certification for an agentic platform (27 Aug 2026, after 900+ technical tests), which show firms certifying their own AI under standards that AIUC says operationalise the EU AI Act among other frameworks (KPMG-ISO-2025; AIUC-KPMG-2026). These are self-reported by KPMG and AIUC.
- **Not found.** Official guidance on whether auditors' AI tools are "high-risk", or any EU audit-oversight body (CEAOB or national) statement on the AI Act. A national authority (Netherlands AFM) published building blocks for controlled use of AI audit tools, listed in IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.11; I could not open the AFM page (landscape: 404).

### 2.7 Professional liability and insurance

- **Negligence principles.** DAC Beachcroft (13 Jul 2026) expects traditional professional negligence principles to apply: an erroneous AI result is "unlikely, by itself" to be a defence or lower the standard of care, and auditors will need to show reasonable due diligence, validation and monitoring. It lists likely triggers: unreviewed reliance, un-investigated AI-flagged anomalies, excessive reliance on automated conclusions, staff who do not understand tool limits, and weak governance or testing. It expects liability not to shift to the technology provider because of exculpatory terms. It states this "has yet to be tested" (DAC-AUDITAI-2026, "Negligence and Professional Liability Risks").
- **What regulators will look for in an investigation.** The same firm expects the FRC to look for records of why a tool was selected, pre-deployment testing, extent of use, review of outputs, and why the team judged results reliable; enquiries are likely to extend into the firm's quality management (DAC-AUDITAI-2026, "Regulatory Exposure and Enforcement Risk"). This is a law firm's expectation, not an FRC statement.
- **US view.** A June 2026 white paper by a US law firm identifies GenAI as an added liability layer (reliance without verification; confidential data in AI platforms) engaging due care, competence, confidentiality and quality management. It recommends written policy, human review, data-input limits, vendor diligence, training and monitoring, noting these controls help show AI was "a tool, not a substitute" (HEPLER-LIAB-2026, "Generative AI Risks"; "Generative AI Governance and Risk Mitigation").
- **Mock-juror evidence.** A study by Libby and Witz (2024), summarised in *Current Issues in Auditing*, found that use of AI can reduce the effect of appearance-of-independence conflicts on juror negligence assessments (CITEDRIVE-LIBBY-2024; summary page only, full paper not read). It is a mock-juror experiment, not case outcomes.
- **Insurance.** In a 26 Jun 2026 trade article, underwriters at several insurers said standard E&O generally covers AI-related negligence in professional services unless a policy has a specific AI exclusion; some said claims remain limited but that more claims could prompt exclusions; some said competitive pressure makes underwriters reluctant to add exclusions; one said hallucination sanctions on lawyers are not insured (BUSINESSINS-AIEO-2026). The article is about professionals generally, with law as the main example. It says nothing specific to audit firms.
- **Search-only (not opened).** A search summary reported that some carriers have introduced broad AI exclusions in professional liability forms and that Verisk issued generative AI exclusion endorsements for general liability in January 2026, with language reported to be adapted into professional lines. Those pages were not opened (the Fenwick page returned 403) and are leads, not facts for this file.
- **No reported case.** My searches found no reported lawsuit that turns on generative AI in an audit (search result summary; DAC-AUDITAI-2026 itself says untested).
- **Firm-structure liability.** The US white paper also lists successor exposure from firm acquisitions as a growing risk, relevant where AI-driven consolidation continues (HEPLER-LIAB-2026, "M&A and Successor Exposure").

### 2.8 Steps that must keep human judgment

Convergent statements across regulators; none of them lists a closed set of "human-only" steps. The common structure is accountability plus defined control points.

- **Accountability and opinion.** FRC (FRC-NEWS-2026); NFRA, "the opinion belongs to an identified auditor" (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026); AICPA (JOFA-AIPA-2026); IFIAR: "audits will remain human-led", and firms should be able to explain what that means in practice (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.7).
- **Defined control points.** IFIAR asks firms to assign specific engagement personnel to decision checkpoints, perform an overarching review of autonomous decisions, and ensure accountability for all work (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.7). The FRC says control points are chosen by professional judgement based on main risks of deficient output and the audit-quality risk if a deficiency is missed; oversight points can be dynamic, triggered when a tool escalates (FRC-GENAI-2026, "Human in the loop review and oversight"; illustrative example 2).
- **Competent reviewers and independent view.** FRC reviewers need competence to spot deficiencies, should apply skepticism and know automation bias, and review "may involve forming an independent view", for example reading the source material behind a summary or identifying risks of material misstatement themselves (FRC-GENAI-2026, "Review", p.37).
- **Skepticism and automation bias.** Named in the IESBA Code as a bias that can impair objectivity (IESBA-SNAPSHOT-2026, Q4); NFRA says skepticism cannot be delegated and warns against fluent output prompting less challenge (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026); AICPA ethics staff list automation, overconfidence and anchoring bias (JOFA-ETHAI-2026); the PCAOB leadership flagged erosion of skepticism and junior skill-building (NYSSCPA-BOTIC-2025).
- **Judgements named as non-delegable or high-risk by one regulator.** NFRA differentiates low-risk tools such as translation aids from higher-risk "agentic tools that select and test samples", which should not get identical scrutiny (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026, "Risk based governance"). The FRC's misuse examples include treating an AI risk-assessment output as a conclusion instead of an input to judgement, and treating a lease-finding summary as complete (FRC-GENAI-2026, "Misinterpretation of output risk"; "Misunderstanding of methodology risk", p.18).
- **Ethics threshold.** The IESBA Code requires accountants using technology outputs to assess fitness for purpose, limitations and assumptions, and data quality; "relying on opaque or unexplained outputs is not acceptable" (IESBA-SNAPSHOT-2026, Q4).
- **Quality management.** An AI tool being used on an engagement does not change the partner's responsibilities for direction, supervision and review; PCAOB staff recorded that firms said their supervision and review policies had not changed for GenAI (PCAOB-GENAI-2024, p.5; staff outreach summary).
- **Clearly outside current regulator statements.** None of the opened sources says specific steps such as going concern conclusions, opinion forming, or engagement quality review must be human-only. The AICPA practice aid says one AI tool may check another without reducing the auditor's duties (landscape R20, not re-opened this run).

### 2.9 Independence, ownership and the AI vendor relationship (regulatory thread)

- **SEC.** OCA is reviewing independence rules: first a refresh of independence FAQs and public sharing of staff answers to consultation questions, then, with the PCAOB, the interim independence standards; Hohl says "nothing could be further from the truth" than weakening them (TR-HOHL-2026, 4 Jun 2026 USC conference). He told a KPMG interviewer that independence rules must remain fit for purpose "particularly" when AI is integrated into audit service delivery (KPMG-HOHL-2026, answer on AI and independence; text read from a copy saved by another agent in the shared scratch directory, page re-fetch returned no text).
- **PCAOB.** An independence standard-setting project, with a proposal anticipated Q1 2027 (PCAOB-SSR-PAGE-2026).
- **AICPA PEEC.** Second exposure draft on alternative practice structures being prepared for an interim October 2026 meeting; revisions considered to "network firm" and a new interpretation prohibiting attest client investment in firms (JOFA-PEEC-2026). The AICPA ethics article of 6 Apr 2026 warns that tool-provider contract terms that shift control, judgment, financial dependency, promotion or evidence access can create independence and objectivity threats in SOC 2 work, and "may also be relevant to other types of audit and attest services" (JOFA-SOCETH-2026, opening paragraphs). For AI audit tool vendors the analogy is a lead rather than a rule.
- **AICPA on AI-related non-attest services.** Developing or hosting an AI system for an attest client can raise independence concerns under existing non-attest and information-systems rules (JOFA-ETHAI-2026, "AI services can raise independence concerns").
- **UK.** The FRC's Sep 2024 letter says it is "not in principle against" external private capital in audit firms, expects firms to engage early and with "full candour", and is concerned with independence and public interest outcomes (FRC-PCLETTER-2024, pp.1-2). The PCAOB's leaders said in Dec 2025 that private equity "may, over time, begin to shift firm incentives so that profitability outweighs audit quality" (NYSSCPA-BOTIC-2025).
- **IESBA.** IESBA's 2023 technology revisions reinforce independence standards for self-review threats, assuming management responsibility and commercial dependencies in technology-enabled services (IESBA-SNAPSHOT-2026, Q4). A March 2026 JofA report said IESBA would assess the need for standards on alternative practice structures (JOFA-IESBAAPS-2026 via landscape-sibling list; not opened this run).

### 2.10 SOC and other attest

- The AICPA published TQA section 9561 on 11 Sep 2026 on the effect of a service organisation's use of AI on SOC 1 and SOC 2 examinations (DART-TQA-2026 via landscape; full text behind login, not read). The practice aid release also references it (AICPA-PA-2026; JOFA-AIPA-2026).
- The PRB's SOC 2 reviewer alert (May 2026) and the ethics article (6 Apr 2026) show that the firm's use of platforms is itself an inspection and ethics matter (JOFA-PEER-2026; JOFA-SOCETH-2026).
- The AICPA's attestation exposure draft proposes standards for sustainability examination and review engagements (new AT-C 325 and 330), effective for engagements beginning on or after 15 Jun 2029 if adopted (CPAPA-ASB-2026).
- COSO's GenAI guidance applies to ICFR-type control design over AI in finance (JOFA-COSO-2026). The AICPA practice aid's first part addresses AI in the entity's controls (AICPA-PA-2026).
- Assurance over AI is emerging as a service: CSA's guidelines for CPAs providing STAR for AI attestation exist (CSA-CPAGUIDE-2026, landing page only); I did not open further detail.

## 3. What it means for an AI transformation conversation

### 3.1 Implications for a leader

1. **Regulation is not the blocker; evidence of governance is.** The regulators that have spoken invite adoption (FRC sandbox, IFIAR "prioritize wider deployment" of high-impact tools) and ask for certification, monitoring, documentation and human control points (FRC-SANDBOX-2026; IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.4). A leader whose firm says "regulation stops us" should be asked which document says so. The PCAOB staff recorded in 2024 that firms saw standards as "not currently viewed as impediments" (PCAOB-GENAI-2024, p.4).
2. **The unit of regulatory attention is the system of quality management.** Tool certification, recertification on model updates, usage controls, training, monitoring metrics and escalation pathways are what the FRC, IFIAR, CPAB and NFRA list (FRC-GENAI-2026, certification and recertification sections; IFIAR-TTF-2026, pp.6-9; CPAB-EVOLVING-2025, p.3; NFRA-CAALLEY-2026). A transformation plan with no owner for these is incomplete.
3. **The standards give no safe harbour for LLM-based evidence.** The IAASB chose not to define input-output tests for tools (IAASB-ED500-2026, EM para 68). Firms therefore carry the methodology risk the FRC calls "non-compliant methodology" (FRC-GENAI-2026, p.19).
4. **Size matters.** The FRC sandbox is open to firms of any size and the FRC expects smaller firms to use third-party tools; the AICPA peer review alert shows how smaller firms can be exposed through platform dependence (FRC-SANDBOX-2026; JOFA-PEER-2026). Third-party tool users face a documentation problem: they may not obtain development history, so the FRC points to independent assurance (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, pp.13-15).
5. **Ownership and AI-vendor relationships are an independence topic**, with SEC, PCAOB and AICPA all moving (see 2.9). Leaders contemplating private capital or a vendor with equity, fee-sharing or control features should treat independence advice as a gating item.
6. **Client data handling is a firm-by-firm judgement today.** There is no common audit-sector standard; the best specific tests are the AICPA ethics staff's consent questions and NFRA's vendor-contract list (JOFA-ETHAI-2026; NFRA-CAALLEY-2026).
7. **The client's AI is now audit work.** Acceptance, risk assessment, controls and evidence reliability are affected when the audited entity uses AI in reporting (AICPA-PA-2026; IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.10; CAQ-PULSE-2026 via landscape: 6% of audit partners rate their largest client's AI governance as advanced).

### 3.2 Interview probes

1. "Which of your tools are certified, by whom, and what triggers recertification when a vendor updates the model?" (FRC-GENAI-2026 recertification list; IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.6.)
2. "If a regulator asked for one tool on one engagement, could you show what was on the file versus what sits centrally?" (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, pp.13-16.)
3. "Who owns monitoring: the network, your member firm or the engagement team? What metrics do you track (false positive rates against manual samples, rework, escalations)?" (IFIAR-TTF-2026, pp.8-9.)
4. "What client data can staff put into which AI tool today, and who approved that against your confidentiality obligations and engagement terms?" (JOFA-ETHAI-2026; NFRA-CAALLEY-2026.)
5. "Which review steps do you require a human to perform from scratch, and how do you test that reviewers are not just accepting fluent output?" (FRC-GENAI-2026, p.37; NFRA-CAALLEY-2026.)
6. "Does any tool vendor have a financial, control or evidence-access relationship that your independence team has reviewed?" (JOFA-SOCETH-2026; JOFA-PEEC-2026.)
7. "What did your last professional indemnity renewal say about AI, and did you answer application questions about AI use?" (BUSINESSINS-AIEO-2026: exclusions can arrive in application questions or endorsements; the article does not say this for audit, so treat as an exploratory probe.)
8. "Do you operate in the EU, and if so who has decided whether any AI tool you use for staffing or performance falls under the AI Act's employment category?" (AIACT-ANNEX3, item 4; inference noted in 2.6.)

### 3.3 Objections and evidence-based responses

| Objection | Response with evidence |
|---|---|
| "The standards don't cover AI, so we can't use it." | The FRC says the guidance supports innovation and the FRC and IAASB have not prohibited anything (FRC-NEWS-2026; IAASB-ED500-2026, EM para 69). PCAOB staff recorded firms saying standards were not impediments (PCAOB-GENAI-2024, p.4). The gap is methodology and evidence, not permission. |
| "Regulators will punish us if the AI is wrong." | The FRC says its guidance is not a response to identified deficiencies (FRC-NEWS-2026). What regulators say they test is governance and judgement: certification, monitoring, documentation and challenge (IFIAR-TTF-2026, pp.6-9). CPAB's finding concerned limited input validation and no testing of low-risk-rated items, not AI use as such (CPAB-EVOLVING-2025, p.1). |
| "A tool that tests 100% of items replaces sampling and reduces the need for judgement." | NFRA says full-population testing does not remove the need for substantive risk assessment and low-risk classifications should be periodically tested (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026). The FRC says whole-population insight on individual items may be less conclusive (FRC-GENAI-2026, p.19). CPAB found no further testing of low-risk-rated items in one tool (CPAB-EVOLVING-2025, p.1). |
| "Documenting AI use will bury the file." | The FRC explicitly asks for proportionate documentation, with more held centrally the more widely a tool is used, and says LLM outputs corroborated to sources may need less (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, pp.6-7, 16). |
| "Our vendor is certified, so we are covered." | NFRA says a firm cannot rely on vendor terms "without validation", and wants independent evaluation of vendor accuracy claims (NFRA-CAALLEY-2026). The FRC says third-party tools still need the same documented rationale, with independent assurance as one route (FRC-AIAUDIT-2025, pp.13-15). Firm certification (for example KPMG's ISO 42001, AIUC-1) covers that firm's platform, not yours (KPMG-ISO-2025; AIUC-KPMG-2026). |
| "Insurance will cover it." | Trade-press underwriters say standard E&O generally covers AI-related negligence unless excluded, and that exclusions may follow claims growth. This is not audit-specific; check the actual wording at renewal (BUSINESSINS-AIEO-2026). |
| "The EU AI Act will not touch us." | Possibly true for audit tools as such: Annex III has no audit category. But AI literacy (Article 4) applies to deployers and HR-type tools may fall under employment, and clients may fall into high-risk categories from Dec 2027 (AIACT-ANNEX3; GIBSONDUNN-AIOMNIBUS-2026). |
| "Human in the loop is enough." | Regulators say it must be specified. CPAB says firms' statements that audits remain human-led are not enough without a behaviour shift and defined involvement at each stage (CPAB-EVOLVING-2025, p.5). IFIAR asks for named decision-checkpoint owners (IFIAR-TTF-2026, p.7). |
