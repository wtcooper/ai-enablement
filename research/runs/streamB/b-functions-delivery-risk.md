# Function cards research: service-delivery and risk-compliance-legal

Run: streamB, brief `b-functions-delivery-risk`. Researched 2026-10-20. Scope: in-house functions in any organization. Firms that sell legal, audit or tax services are the professional-services, assurance and tax archetypes and are cited by id only.

Conventions. Refs are `PUBLISHER-SHORT-YYYY` (section 7). Grades follow the vocabulary (A measured outcome reported by the named organization; B named deployment without published measurement, or a recollection or rough estimate; C vendor claim or analyst estimate; D idea). Provenance is `self-reported`, `vendor-published` or `independent`. Where a source marks its own figure as an estimate, or a participant estimated it from memory, the grade is capped at B and the entry says so. "Government evaluation" sources are typed `regulator` (public body) in the source list because the vocabulary has no separate government type.

Process names in the item profiles are the analyst's own synthesis (no source ref). I opened only the landing page of the APQC process framework (APQC-PCF-2026) and did not copy its category names. No framework ids are used.

## 1. Summary

- **Measured, comparison-group evidence for AI in in-house operations comes mostly from public bodies, because they publish evaluations.** The UK Home Office asylum pilots (23 and 37 minutes per case against comparison groups, sustained-decision rates similar) and the UK i.AI consultation tool (reviewers left 73% of mappings unchanged; two human reviewers agreed with each other less than the tool agreed with a reviewer) are the best-measured examples found. Private-sector operations examples (Danfoss, DHL, Siemens) are vendor-published or press-release level, with estimates and no quality measures.
- **The same task can go both ways, so test on the organization's own work first.** An Australian regulator's proof of concept scored AI summaries of public submissions at 47% against 81% for human summaries (ASIC-SENATE-2024), while the Home Office summaries saved time with a similar sustained-decision rate in test and comparison groups (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025). Randomized studies show the same unevenness: quality gains in four of six legal tasks but not in the transactional drafting task (UMN-SCHWARCZ-2025 via LAWNEXT-2025); 19% fewer correct answers on a task outside the frontier (HBS-DELLACQUA-2023).
- **Time-saved figures are mostly self-estimates and shrink under controlled design.** The UK cross-government Copilot trial reports 26 minutes a day from a user survey (GDS-COPILOT-2025); HMRC's randomized-licence phase reports 2-3% of the working week, and HMRC itself calls its figures a baseline estimate (HMRC-COPILOT-2026). McKinsey's 2026 survey finds 80% report personal productivity gains but 37% report any enterprise EBIT impact, flat on the year (MCKINSEY-STATEAI-2026).
- **In risk and compliance the strongest measured result is a before-and-after on a high-volume screening step; the strongest warnings are about autonomy.** HSBC reports a 12-month pre/post comparison for AML monitoring (HSBC-GOOGLE-2023). Michigan's automated fraud system (93% error rate, about 40,000 people wrongly accused, per a university summary of the case) and DWP's own fairness assessment (higher referral rates for some age groups that were not matched by higher hit rates) show why a person who decides, and a way to measure the model, are design requirements (UMICH-MIDAS-2024; DWP-UCADV-2025).
- **Regulators are asking for evidence of control, not for new AI-specific rules.** The US DOJ asks what "baseline of human decision-making" is used to assess AI and how fast the company can detect and correct AI decisions (DOJ-ECP-2024); the FCA says it does not plan extra AI regulation (FCA-AIAPPROACH-2025); FINRA lists testing, prompt and output logging and human review (FINRA-GENAI-2025); the EU AI Act asks deployers to give oversight to people with competence, training and authority (EUAIACT-ART26-2024).
- **In-house legal is the best-surveyed risk function, and the surveys point to insourcing.** the ACC/Everlaw survey (657 respondents, 30 countries) reports GenAI usage in corporate law "more than doubles in a single year", 64% expect to rely less on outside counsel and 91% cite efficiency gains (ACC-EVERLAW-2025, vendor co-sponsored; year-on-year comparisons are US only). Independent accuracy tests of legal research tools find hallucination between 17% and 33% for three leading products in 2024 (STANFORD-MAGESH-2024), while a 2025 third-party benchmark finds four AI products scoring above a lawyer baseline on research questions (VALS-VLAIR-2025).
- **Redesigned workflows, not tools, separate the few organizations that report enterprise value.** About three-quarters of McKinsey's "high performers" (about 6% of respondents) report redesigning workflows because of AI, against one quarter of others (MCKINSEY-STATEAI-2026). Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027 (GARTNER-AGENTIC-2025, analyst prediction).
- **Quality evidence is thin where it matters most.** No source found publishes override or escalation rates for in-house legal or compliance AI, and no parallel run of an in-house legal team. The few published quality measures are pilot rejection rates (9% of asylum summaries removed after review), reviewer agreement baselines (Consult), and the DWP fairness figures.

## 2. Item profiles

### 2.1 `service-delivery` (in-house service delivery and operations)

**What the function is.** The in-house team that produces and delivers the organization's core service or product to customers, citizens or patients: fulfilment, case and application processing, site or field operations, back-office operations and the quality control around them. The library defines it as "Service delivery and operations". Customer-facing contact is the `customer-service` card; people, finance, procurement and IT operations have their own cards.

**Main processes and key steps, with work types** (analyst synthesis; no source):

| Process | Key steps | Work types |
|---|---|---|
| Demand and capacity planning | Forecast volumes; set staffing and schedules; reallocate across queues | `coordination-scheduling`, `data-reporting` |
| Intake and registration | Receive orders, requests or cases by email, form, call or system feed; capture data; check completeness | `transaction-processing`, `client-interaction` |
| Triage, routing and dispatch | Classify; prioritise; assign to person, team or site; book appointments | `coordination-scheduling`, `transaction-processing` |
| Fulfilment or case work | Gather information; assess against rules or policy; decide; produce the output; deliver | `expert-judgment`, `transaction-processing`, `document-production`, `physical-field` |
| Exceptions and escalations | Detect breaks; investigate; get approval; resolve; communicate | `expert-judgment`, `transaction-processing` |
| Quality assurance and rework | Sample or check outputs; record findings; correct; feed back | `review-assurance` |
| Performance monitoring and reporting | Track cycle time, backlog, quality, cost; report; trigger action | `data-reporting` |
| Improvement and partner coordination | Root-cause analysis; process change; manage outsourced or partner work | `coordination-scheduling`, `research-synthesis` |

**Typical pain points** (with source):
- Orders and requests arrive as unstructured email and need manual entry across several systems. At Danfoss, a digital ordering tool for customers outside its system integrations "often required as much manual input as email-based ordering", and sales and support staff worked across up to five systems (GOOGLE-DANFOSS-2026, Challenge section and Results panel).
- Case preparation is document-heavy. UK asylum decision-makers read interview transcripts that "can extend to 50 pages or more" and search country policy notes by hand (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §1.1-1.2).
- High-volume text analysis is slow and costly. The UK government runs around 600 public consultations a year, some with more than 100,000 responses, and analysing them takes "hundreds of thousands of hours" or is contracted out (IAI-CONSULT-2025, Introduction p.2).
- Routine operational communication takes staff time. DHL Supply Chain names manual data entry, routine scheduling and standardized communications as work its agents take on (DHL-HAPPYROBOT-2025, CIO quote).
- Documentation burden on frontline professionals: Kaiser Permanente's Northern California medical group offered an AI scribe to reduce note-taking burden (KP-TPMG-2025, para 1 and 4).
- Value does not reach the enterprise level. 37% of McKinsey's 2026 respondents attribute some EBIT impact to AI, essentially unchanged from 2025; respondents most often report cost reductions from supply chain management, service operations and manufacturing (MCKINSEY-STATEAI-2026, Key takeaways and Exhibit 6).
- Pilots do not reach production. MIT NANDA's preliminary study (interviews with 52 organizations, 153 leader survey responses, 300+ public initiatives) reports that only 5% of enterprise-grade task-specific tools reached production, and that the highest returns it documented were in back-office functions (MIT-NANDA-2025, executive summary, §3.2 and back-office section). It is a preliminary report with anonymized data.

**Metrics and benchmarks** (what each measures, who measured it):
- Time per case: Home Office ACS pilot, test group reviewed transcripts 23 minutes quicker (32% time saving) against a comparison group; APS pilot 37 minutes per case; case logging by participants, evaluated by the Home Office (self-reported; HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §3.1, §4).
- Decision quality as a control: share of sustained decisions in a dip sample, using the Home Office's own quality tool, similar between test and comparison groups (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §4.1-4.2). Sample sizes for the dip sample are not stated.
- Rejection rate of AI output before use: 9% of ACS summaries were judged inaccurate or incomplete by technical specialists and removed; 23% of users of the remaining summaries were not fully confident; 42% felt the summary gave the right amount of information (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §5.1; survey of 26 respondents, 43% response rate, §3.2).
- Agreement with human reviewers, with a human-to-human baseline: Consult F1 0.816 against a single reviewer; 0.710 between two reviewers; reviewers made no change to 73% of Consult mappings; two reviewers gave identical mappings for 65% of responses; 87,738 responses reviewed by 125 people; median review time 19 seconds per response (IAI-CONSULT-2025, Executive summary and Findings; i.AI is a UK government unit, so self-reported).
- Rubric score against human work: AI summaries 35 of 75 points (47%), human summaries 61 of 75 (81%); one model (Llama2-70B) at one time on one use case; a five-week proof of concept run by AWS for ASIC (ASIC-SENATE-2024, Phase 3 final assessment and Limitations).
- Self-estimated time saved: 26 minutes a day (cross-government Copilot trial, 7,115 survey responses, computed from range mid-points, GDS-COPILOT-2025, §4); HMRC 2-3% of the working week, about 60 minutes a week for a full-time equivalent, reduced by about 20% for non-use (HMRC-COPILOT-2026, Results item 2). HMRC says the figures are "a baseline estimate rather than a precise measure".
- Cycle time and automation share (vendor-published, estimates): Danfoss reports "up to 80%" of transactional decision-making and communication automated and customer response time "potentially" from 42 hours to near real time; the team estimates about five minutes saved per order (GOOGLE-DANFOSS-2026, Results panel and Danfoss quote).
- Documentation hours: 7,260 TPMG physicians used the scribe in 2,576,627 encounters between October 2023 and December 2024; the analysis found nearly 16,000 hours of documentation time saved (KP-TPMG-2025, paras 1 and 6).

**Guardrails and decisions that need human judgment:**
- Keep the decision with the person. The Home Office designed both tools so decision-makers "cannot use the tool by itself to make a decision", and APS requires access to the full policy note (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §1.1-1.2).
- Decide what the person must see. The Home Office summaries did not give source references in the pilot, which some users said limited time saved (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §4.1 and §5.1). Citations are a design choice that changes review effort.
- Exceptions stay with staff. Danfoss staff still handle more complex transactions such as pricing exceptions (GOOGLE-DANFOSS-2026, "Turning transactional data" section).
- Watch for over-reliance. Bainbridge's 1983 paper argues that automation leaves operators the hard residual tasks and erodes the skill needed for them (BAINBRIDGE-1983, §1). The EU AI Act asks that people overseeing high-risk systems "remain aware" of the tendency to over-rely on output (EUAIACT-ART14-2024, Art. 14(4)(b)).
- Post-deployment monitoring needs mechanisms for user input, "appeal and override", incident response and change management (NIST-AIRMF-2023, MANAGE 4.1). Pilots should record who overrode what.
- Classify the use. AI used by public authorities to evaluate eligibility for essential public benefits is a high-risk category in the EU AI Act (EUAIACT-ANNEX3-2024, Annex III point 5(a)); most internal operations uses are not listed, so the check is per use.
- Equality and sub-group checks need larger samples than pilots give. The Home Office said pilot numbers were too small to test variation across all characteristics (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §4.1).

**How roles change:**
- Novices gain most from assistance in a customer-support field study (34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers, minimal impact on experienced workers) (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023, abstract); this is adjacent evidence from customer service.
- Reviewers become verifiers. Consult reviewers checked and amended each mapping; the Home Office decision-makers read a summary and then decide (IAI-CONSULT-2025, §How Consult works; HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §1.1).
- Single interface and fewer systems: Danfoss staff moved from up to five systems to one interface; its Head of Digital Business says staff focus on "value-added activities" and reports faster onboarding of new hires (GOOGLE-DANFOSS-2026, Results panel and García quote).
- DHL says it aims to make operational roles "more engaging and rewarding" by automating repetitive tasks (DHL-HAPPYROBOT-2025, CIO quote). This is a stated aim.
- Strain is uneven: in McKinsey's 2026 survey 47% of mid-level managers and individual contributors report an AI-related strain, against 31% of executives and senior managers (MCKINSEY-STATEAI-2026, "AI impact remains concentrated").

### 2.2 `risk-compliance-legal` (in-house risk, compliance and legal)

**What the function is.** In-house teams that identify and manage risk, keep the organization within law and policy, and provide legal advice and contracts. In financial services this includes financial-crime compliance; in the public sector it includes payment integrity and fraud control. Firms that sell legal, audit or tax services are covered by other archetypes.

**Main processes and key steps, with work types** (analyst synthesis; no source):

| Process | Key steps | Work types |
|---|---|---|
| Risk identification and assessment | Maintain register; assess likelihood and impact; set appetite; report | `data-reporting`, `expert-judgment` |
| Policy and control framework | Draft and update policies; map controls to obligations; attest | `document-production`, `review-assurance` |
| Regulatory change and obligations | Monitor changes; interpret; map to policies and controls; brief owners | `research-synthesis`, `expert-judgment` |
| Compliance monitoring and testing | Screen transactions or communications; test controls; investigate alerts; close or escalate | `transaction-processing`, `review-assurance`, `data-reporting` |
| Customer and third-party due diligence | Collect documents; screen; score risk; approve or reject; re-screen | `transaction-processing`, `research-synthesis` |
| Contract lifecycle | Intake request; draft from template; review against playbook; negotiate; sign; store; track obligations | `document-production`, `review-assurance`, `expert-judgment` |
| Legal advice, research and matters | Receive question; research; advise; manage outside counsel and matters | `expert-judgment`, `research-synthesis`, `coordination-scheduling` |
| Investigations, incidents and regulator engagement | Open case; gather evidence; decide; report; remediate | `expert-judgment`, `coordination-scheduling`, `document-production` |
| Governance of the organization's own AI | Inventory uses; approve tools; set oversight; monitor; report incidents | `review-assurance`, `coordination-scheduling` |

**Typical pain points** (with source):
- False positives swamp investigators. HSBC screens "over 1.2 billion transactions" a month and says its rules-based approach produced many false positives that needed manual review (HSBC-GOOGLE-2023, opening and "Re-defining" sections). A 2025 vendor-linked survey of 20 banks and broker-dealers reportedly found over 70% had surveillance false-positive rates above 25% (search-only: EVENTUS-DATOS-2025).
- Wrong or invented legal content creates risk. Three leading legal research tools hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time in one preregistered test (STANFORD-MAGESH-2024, abstract); courts are recording cases of fabricated or misrepresented citations (CHARLOTIN-2026).
- Volume and turnaround. Luminance's own legal team handled "over 50 contracts each day" (TECHUK-LUMINANCE-2024, Challenge).
- Cost of outside counsel. 50% of surveyed in-house professionals expect AI to cut outside counsel costs, and 64% expect to rely less on outside counsel (ACC-EVERLAW-2025, bullet "Reliance on outside counsel").
- Training and policy gaps. 64% of corporate professionals in a 2025 survey had received no GenAI training, and about a third of legal and risk professionals were unsure whether GenAI should be used in their field (TR-GENAI-2025, Key findings for Corporate Professionals). The share saying company policy prohibits GenAI use fell from 29% to 9% in the ACC survey (ACC-EVERLAW-2025).
- Leaders' concern about the pace of adoption: a 2023 Gartner survey of 179 legal, compliance and privacy leaders found about 70% ranked rapid GenAI adoption their top issue (search-only: GARTNER-LEGAL-2023).

**Top AI opportunities** (ladder, change scope, evidence):

| # | Opportunity | Ladder | Scope | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ML scoring of transactions to replace rules-based monitoring; investigators work the alerts | automate | step | A: HSBC (RC-1) |
| 2 | ML prioritisation and check-fraud identification in public payment integrity | automate | step | A: US Treasury (RC-2); method not published |
| 3 | Fraud-risk referral model with a person always deciding, control-group referrals and routine fairness analysis | augment | step | A: DWP UC Advances (RC-3); shows both effect and limits |
| 4 | In-house contract first-pass review and redlining inside the drafting tool, lawyer approves each change | augment | step | B: ITG (RC-5), Luminance (RC-4). Randomized evidence on law students supports quality and speed gains for litigation-type tasks, not for NDA drafting (UMN-SCHWARCZ-2025) |
| 5 | Template-based contract generation by non-lawyers and a legal helpdesk | automate | process | B: Luminance (RC-4); archetype entry professional-services-acceptance-risk-14 (B) |
| 6 | Grounded legal research assistant with citations | assist, augment | step | Measured in studies, not as named in-house deployments: STANFORD-MAGESH-2024, VALS-VLAIR-2025, UMN-SCHWARCZ-2025 |
| 7 | Regulatory-change monitoring, policy gap analysis, risk-register upkeep, vendor assessment | augment | step | Archetype entries (below), mostly C and B; no cross-industry measured example found |
| 8 | Move work from outside counsel to the in-house team | augment | model | C: ACC-EVERLAW-2025 expectation (78% expect to insource drafting, 71% contract management, 62% research); survey of expectations, vendor co-sponsored |
| 9 | Automated fraud-case identification and interception at scale with limited oversight | automate | process | Negative evidence: Michigan MiDAS (RC-6) |

**Metrics and benchmarks:**
- Detection and alert load, HSBC: AML AI identifies two to four times as much suspicious activity as the previous system while reducing alerts by 60%; "twice as much identified financial crime" in commercial banking and "almost four times" in retail banking; suspicious accounts detected "down to eight days after the first alert". Footnote: comparison of the 12 months after go-live with the preceding 12 months of rules-based monitoring, adjusting for seasonality and excluding the first month after go-live (self-reported, written by an HSBC financial-crime executive and published on a vendor's blog; HSBC-GOOGLE-2023, "Re-defining" section and footnote 1).
- Payment integrity: Treasury's Office of Payment Integrity reports over $4 billion prevented and recovered in FY2024 against $652.7 million in FY2023, including $1 billion recovery from machine-learning check-fraud identification and $2.5 billion prevention from identifying and prioritising high-risk transactions (self-reported; the release does not give methods; TREASURY-FISCAL-2024, Highlights).
- Referral accuracy by group: DWP finds that for some age groups an increased likelihood of referral by the model is inconsistent with a reduced likelihood that referrals were correct, and says the model "is not working as effectively as we would expect"; the median impact on payment of an advance that was referred and later approved was 1 day (self-reported; DWP-UCADV-2025, paras 21-24 and the conclusions summary).
- Legal research accuracy: Lexis+ AI answered 65% of queries accurately in one preregistered test of over 200 queries, Westlaw AI-Assisted Research 42%; hallucination between 17% and 33% (independent; STANFORD-MAGESH-2024, abstract and introduction). A 2025 third-party benchmark of 200 research questions scored four AI products 74%-78% weighted against 69% for a lawyer baseline (VALS-VLAIR-2025, executive summary).
- Productivity in legal tasks (randomized, law students): productivity gains of about 38% to 115% (Vincent) and 34% to 140% (o1-preview) in five of six tasks (UMN-SCHWARCZ-2025, abstract; LAWNEXT-2025).
- In-house adoption: the survey headline says GenAI usage in corporate law "more than doubles in a single year"; 61% of European respondents already use GenAI in legal practice; 657 respondents across 30 countries; year-on-year comparisons are US only (ACC-EVERLAW-2025; vendor co-sponsored survey). A press summary I saw only in search results gives 52% actively using, up from 23% (search-only).
- Financial-sector AI use: 75% of surveyed UK financial firms already use AI, 55% of AI use cases have some automated decision-making, 24% semi-autonomous, 2% fully autonomous (independent; BOE-FCA-AISURVEY-2024, Executive summary and §2.5).
- Review-time baseline: LegalOn's December 2025 survey of 452 in-house professionals reportedly puts average review at 3.1 hours per contract (search-only; vendor survey; not usable for an A or B grade).

**Guardrails and decisions that need human judgment:**
- Evidence of control. DOJ prosecutors ask whether controls monitor AI "trustworthiness, reliability" and lawful use, what "baseline of human decision-making is used to assess AI", how accountability is enforced, and "how quickly can the company detect and correct decisions made by AI" (DOJ-ECP-2024, §I.A p.4 and §III.A p.18).
- Securities supervision. FINRA says its rules apply to GenAI as to any technology; firms using GenAI in supervision may consider model integrity, reliability and accuracy; it lists testing, prompt and output logs, model-version tracking and human-in-the-loop review, and flags agent risks of autonomy without validation, acting beyond scope, and hard-to-trace reasoning (FINRA-GENAI-2025, Regulatory Obligations, Emerging Trends, Agents).
- UK approach. The FCA says it does not plan extra regulation for AI and relies on existing frameworks such as the Consumer Duty and senior-manager accountability (FCA-AIAPPROACH-2025, "How do our current rules apply?").
- EU AI Act. High-risk systems must be designed so people can effectively oversee them and stay aware of automation bias (EUAIACT-ART14-2024, Art. 14(1), 14(2), 14(4)(b)); deployers assign oversight to people with "competence, training and authority" (EUAIACT-ART26-2024, Art. 26(2)). Annex III lists, among others, creditworthiness assessment (excluding AI used to detect financial fraud), eligibility for essential public benefits, employment decisions and AI used by judicial authorities (EUAIACT-ANNEX3-2024, points 4, 5, 8). Which high-risk obligations apply to a given in-house use needs a case-by-case check.
- Management standards. NIST's AI RMF asks that deployed systems be shown to be valid and reliable with documented limits (MEASURE 2.5) and that post-deployment plans include "appeal and override" (MANAGE 4.1) (NIST-AIRMF-2023, Core tables); its generative AI profile defines confabulation as confidently stated erroneous content (NIST-GENAI-2024, §2.2).
- Claims about AI must be true. The SEC settled charges against two investment advisers for false or misleading statements about their use of AI, with $400,000 in total civil penalties (SEC-2024-36).
- Accountability for AI statements. A British Columbia tribunal held Air Canada liable for a chatbot's incorrect bereavement-fare advice and awarded $650.88 (MCCARTHY-MOFFATT-2024, summary of decision).
- Design a person who really decides. In DWP's model a person always decides; a blend of high-risk and control-group referrals goes to human review, and the reviewing employee does not receive a risk rating or know the referral came from the model (DWP-UCADV-2025, paras 10-11).
- Decisions that stay with people, per the sources: whether to approve or decline a referred claim (DWP-UCADV-2025, para 11); lawyers approve or amend each suggested contract change (QANOONI-ITG-2026, "Evidence-linked drafting"); investigators review alerts (HSBC-GOOGLE-2023, "Re-defining" section).

**How roles change:**
- Investigators spend more time on genuinely suspicious cases: HSBC says the lower alert volume lets investigation teams do this (HSBC-GOOGLE-2023, "Re-defining" section).
- Lawyers move to review and approval of AI-proposed edits with citations, while remaining responsible for approvals (QANOONI-ITG-2026).
- Non-lawyers generate contracts from pre-approved templates, and the legal helpdesk responds in under an hour on average (TECHUK-LUMINANCE-2024, Impact).
- Work moves in-house: 78% of surveyed in-house professionals expect to insource drafting, 71% contract management and 62% research; 61% plan to push for change in how legal services are delivered and priced (ACC-EVERLAW-2025).
- Automation can leave hard residual tasks and reduce practice of the skills needed for them (BAINBRIDGE-1983, §1), which matters for junior reviewers who no longer draft first versions (author's inference, not stated in the source).

## 4. Expert moves and probes

### 4.1 `service-delivery`

1. **"Pick one queue. What are the volume, the handling time per item, the share that needs a person's judgment, and how do you know?"** Reveals whether the team has baselines. The Home Office pilots could measure time and quality because case logs and a quality tool already existed (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §3.1, §4). Without that, the case is built on self-estimates (GDS-COPILOT-2025, §4).
2. **"Show me what a person sees when they review the AI output. Can they trace each statement to the source?"** Reveals the review effort the design implies; the Home Office summaries lacked source references and some users saw minimal time saving (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §4.1).
3. **"What is the human-to-human agreement rate on this work today?"** Reveals whether "accuracy" can be defined at all. Consult's two reviewers matched each other less (0.710) than the tool matched one reviewer (0.816), so a perfect-answer standard would have been the wrong benchmark (IAI-CONSULT-2025, Findings).
4. **"Which exceptions today go to a senior person, and what would you want the system to do instead of guessing?"** Reveals the escalation design. Danfoss leaves pricing exceptions with staff and treats them as a next stage (GOOGLE-DANFOSS-2026).
5. **"If we test a model on your own documents, what would failure look like, and who would score it blind?"** Reveals appetite for a test like ASIC's blind rubric, where AI scored 47% against 81% for humans on a task the vendor considered suitable (ASIC-SENATE-2024).
6. **"Who gets the time back, and what will they do with it?"** The cross-government trial could not show how saved time was spent (GDS-COPILOT-2025, §6), and McKinsey finds individual gains without enterprise EBIT impact for most (MCKINSEY-STATEAI-2026). Reveals whether the capacity has a destination.
7. **"Which of your systems would an agent need to read or write, and who owns each?"** Reveals integration load. Gartner says integrating agents into legacy systems "can be technically complex" and often disrupts workflows (GARTNER-AGENTIC-2025, analyst view).
8. **"What do the newest people on the team do that the experienced ones do differently?"** Reveals where assistance may help most; novices gained most in a support-centre study (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023). Test whether the same holds in the operation, since the study is from customer support.
9. **"If the automation were switched off on Monday, could the team still do the work?"** Reveals skill and monitoring risk raised by Bainbridge (BAINBRIDGE-1983, §1) and the need for a manual fallback.
10. **"What would you do about outsourced or contracted work that AI could do?"** Reveals model-level options and whether vendor contracts allow change. MIT NANDA reports its largest documented savings from ending outsourced back-office spend, though anonymized and interview-based (MIT-NANDA-2025, back-office section).

### 4.2 `risk-compliance-legal`

1. **"Which decisions about a person or a transaction does the team make, and for which does a person have to decide?"** Reveals the autonomy boundary. DWP keeps a person on every decision (DWP-UCADV-2025, para 11); Michigan's automated fraud determinations show the cost of getting this wrong (UMICH-MIDAS-2024).
2. **"What is your alert or review volume, the false-positive rate, and the cost per alert?"** Reveals whether a step-level scoring change (HSBC) has a measurable baseline. HSBC states its method for the 12-month comparison (HSBC-GOOGLE-2023, footnote 1).
3. **"How would you know the model is wrong for some groups or cases?"** Reveals monitoring maturity. DWP's own analysis found referral and hit rates diverging for some age groups (DWP-UCADV-2025, paras 22-23); DOJ asks how quickly the company can "detect and correct" AI decisions (DOJ-ECP-2024, §III.A).
4. **"Do you have an inventory of where AI is used in the function, including inside vendor tools?"** Reveals governance gaps; 33% of AI use cases in UK financial services are third-party implementations (BOE-FCA-AISURVEY-2024, Executive summary), and FINRA expects firms to consider third-party GenAI risk (FINRA-GENAI-2025, General).
5. **"What does your policy say on approved tools, and what training have people had?"** Reveals the policy and training gap seen in surveys (TR-GENAI-2025: 64% untrained; ACC-EVERLAW-2025: 9% report a prohibition).
6. **"Who checks citations and authorities before anything leaves the team, and how is that recorded?"** Reveals verification practice. Hallucination rates of 17-33% in leading research tools (STANFORD-MAGESH-2024) and court-recorded fabricated citations (CHARLOTIN-2026) show why; an accuracy test on the team's own questions is better than a vendor claim.
7. **"Which contract types or questions are routine enough for template generation or a playbook, and which are never to be touched without a lawyer?"** Reveals the split between automation (Luminance helpdesk and templates) and augment (lawyer approves each change, QANOONI-ITG-2026). Randomized evidence found gains on litigation-type tasks but not on an NDA drafting task (LAWNEXT-2025).
8. **"If a regulator or prosecutor asked what baseline of human decision-making you compare the AI against, what would you show?"** Reveals measurement readiness, taken directly from DOJ's question (DOJ-ECP-2024, §I.A p.4).
9. **"What do you tell customers, investors and clients about your AI use, and who verifies those statements?"** Reveals the "AI washing" exposure (SEC-2024-36) and chatbot-statement liability (MCCARTHY-MOFFATT-2024).
10. **"How much outside counsel spend could move in-house, and what would the in-house team stop doing to make room?"** Reveals whether a model-level change is intended; 64% of surveyed in-house professionals expect to rely less on outside counsel (ACC-EVERLAW-2025).

## 5. Evidence on what works and what fails

Provenance in brackets. Absence of evidence is stated where it applies.

**Controlled or comparison-group measurements**
- Home Office asylum pilots, two units each, with comparison groups: time savings of 23 and 37 minutes per case; sustained-decision rates similar; 9% of summaries removed; self-reported and small-scale; "all the evaluation methodologies use self-reporting" [government, self-reported] (HOMEOFFICE-ASYLUM-2025, §3, §4, §7).
- Consult: double review of a live consultation; F1 against a single reviewer 0.816, between reviewers 0.710; reviewers changed nothing for 73% of mappings against 65% identical between two reviewers; no bias by disability status found[government unit, self-reported] (IAI-CONSULT-2025, Executive summary and Findings).
- Customer-support field study, 5,179 agents, staggered introduction: issues resolved per hour up 14% on average and 34% for novice and low-skilled workers, minimal impact for experienced workers; customer sentiment improved [academic, independent] (NBER-BRYNJOLFSSON-2023, abstract). Adjacent to this card.
- Randomized trial with law students, six legal tasks: quality improved in four of six assignments for both tools; quality gains did not extend to the one transactional task (an NDA); accuracy evidence mixed; productivity gains in five of six tasks; o1-preview produced some hallucinations, Vincent roughly the same as no AI [academic, independent]. Participants were students, not in-house lawyers; the published figures changed between versions (UMN-SCHWARCZ-2025 abstract; LAWNEXT-2025).
- Preregistered field experiment, 758 consultants: on 18 tasks inside the AI frontier, 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, better quality; on a task outside the frontier, 19% less likely to produce correct solutions with AI [academic, independent] (HBS-DELLACQUA-2023, abstract). Adjacent: consulting tasks, GPT-4 era.
- Blind rubric scoring against human work (ASIC): 47% against 81% [regulator-commissioned, self-reported via Senate answers] (ASIC-SENATE-2024).
- Preregistered test of legal research tools: hallucination 17% (Lexis+ AI) to 33% (Westlaw AI-Assisted Research); accuracy 65% and 42%; GPT-4 hallucinated more; vendor claims of "hallucination-free" outputs were overstated [academic, independent; 2024 versions of the tools] (STANFORD-MAGESH-2024).
- Third-party benchmark: four AI products at 74%-78% against a 69% lawyer baseline on 200 research questions; legal AI products had higher authoritativeness (citations); some products failed to respond on some questions; weaker on 50-state surveys [third-party benchmark, independent] (VALS-VLAIR-2025, executive summary and key findings).
- Pre/post: HSBC 12-month comparison [self-reported, on vendor blog] (HSBC-GOOGLE-2023).
- Randomized licence allocation at HMRC with survey-based time estimates [self-reported] (HMRC-COPILOT-2026).

**Failures and incident analyses**
- Michigan MiDAS: error rate reached 93%, about 40,000 people wrongly accused, $20 million settlement; agency and state could not produce evidence for the fraud claims when challenged [university summary of court case and state review] (UMICH-MIDAS-2024). Only a secondary source was read.
- DWP UC Advances: referral rates and correct-referral rates diverge for some age groups; model to be retrained [government, self-reported] (DWP-UCADV-2025, paras 22-24). A design lesson is the control-group referral and the blind reviewer.
- Air Canada chatbot: liability for the bot's misstatement; $650.88 [tribunal decision, summarised by a law firm] (MCCARTHY-MOFFATT-2024).
- AI washing: two investment advisers settled with the SEC for false statements about AI use, $400,000 in total penalties [regulator] (SEC-2024-36).
- Courts: a public database lists decisions involving fabricated or misrepresented citations, with lawyers and self-represented litigants among those named; I could not retrieve a total count (CHARLOTIN-2026) [community database, independent].
- Pilot-to-production: MIT NANDA reports 5% of enterprise-grade task-specific tools reaching production (60% evaluated, 20% reached pilot); preliminary report, anonymized, based on 52 interviews, 153 survey responses and 300+ initiatives [academic-led, independent but preliminary] (MIT-NANDA-2025, §3.2). Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end of 2027 for cost, unclear value or inadequate risk controls, and estimates only about 130 of the thousands of agentic vendors are real [analyst prediction] (GARTNER-AGENTIC-2025).
- Value gap: 37% attribute some EBIT impact; high performers about 6%; about 20% of respondents say AI operating costs constrain use [survey, independent but self-reported by respondents] (MCKINSEY-STATEAI-2026).

**What the evidence suggests works** (inference from the above, not stated by one source): a defined step with a reviewer who decides; a baseline and a comparison group; a quality control that already exists; source references on outputs; a human-agreement benchmark; sub-group analysis; and explicit handling of exceptions.

**Absence of evidence.** No source reports override or escalation rates for in-house legal or compliance AI; no parallel run of an in-house legal team against AI; no independent measurement of any contract-review deployment; Danfoss, DHL and Siemens publish no error or rework rates; no published incident reports from in-house compliance AI other than the public-sector and tribunal cases above.
