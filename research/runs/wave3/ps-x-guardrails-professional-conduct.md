# Cross-cutting topic: guardrails and professional conduct (professional-services archetype)

Prepared 2026-10-19. Method: `curl`, `pdftotext` and the r.jina.ai text mirror into a private working folder, plus 11 rationed web searches. Public sources only. Source refs follow `PUBLISHER-SHORT-YYYY` and reuse the landscape's refs where the document is the same. Every source is tagged `full`, `secondary` (the content was read only through a summary or a news report) or `search-only` (seen in search results, not opened). Items about in-house legal teams are tagged **in-house**. Practice-specific audit and tax technical content is kept out; audit-standard items appear only as governance signals and are marked "routes to assurance".

How to read the evidence. This topic has two kinds of source. Regulator and bar texts say what is required or advised. Court records, trackers and insurer commentary say what is happening in practice. Where the two disagree on a number or a date, the entry says so.

## 1. Summary

- **The duties are not new, and the regulators say so.** Bar opinions in California, Texas, DC, New York City and the ABA all apply existing rules (competence, confidentiality, supervision, communication, fees) to generative AI rather than creating new ones (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Exec. Summary p.1; TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, intro; DCBAR-OP388-2024, opening paragraphs; EZEL-ABA512-2024, summary). Consulting's global body took the same line in a May 2026 code (ICMCI-AICODE-2026, s.2.1-2.2).
- **The 2026 shift is from "generative" to "agentic".** California replaced its 2023 guidance on 14 May 2026, at the California Supreme Court's request, and ties the required level of supervision to the system's autonomy (CALBAR-ETHTECH-2026; CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.2-5). The UK Bar Standards Board issued AI guidance on 18 May 2026 (BSB-PR-2026). England's Divisional Court said in June 2025 that heads of chambers and managing partners must take "practical and effective measures" and that courts will ask whether they did (EWHC-AYINDE-2025, para 9).
- **Sanctions are now a large, measurable and still-growing record.** One tracker lists 2,202 court decisions worldwide, including 1,501 in the USA and 76 in the UK, as of 19 October 2026 (CHARLOTIN-DB-2026). Named firms have been hit: K&L Gates and Ellis George ($31,100, May 2025), Butler Snow lawyers removed from a case (July 2025), Sullivan & Cromwell's apology (April 2026) (ABAJ-KLGATES-2025; ABAJ-JOHNSON-2025; ABAJ-SC-2026).
- **Billing rules converge on one point: hourly billers charge for time actually spent, not time saved.** California, Texas, DC and the ABA agree, and California adds that general AI subscriptions are overhead (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.8; TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Fees; DCBAR-OP388-2024, intro; EZEL-ABA512-2024). Clients are using AI adoption as price leverage (TRI-LEGALWEEK-2026).
- **Privilege and confidentiality have a first US ruling.** In *United States v. Heppner* (S.D.N.Y., February 2026) a defendant's own chats with a consumer AI service were held neither privileged nor work product; a different court reached the opposite result for a self-represented litigant (ORRICK-HEPPNER-2026; BROWNING-ARMFOR-2026, "Recent Conflicting Judicial Rulings" slide).
- **Client-side terms are appearing, but public evidence is thin and mostly secondary.** 82% of 468 general counsel surveyed by KPMG expect outside firms to track and share AI use (via ATL-STATS-2026). A consultancy's count found 20 of 1,054 published outside-counsel guidelines mention AI (via HAQQ-CONFID-2026). Canada's procurement agency now requires suppliers to identify AI use in advance (CP-DELOITTECANADA-2025).
- **Insurance is the unsettled backstop.** Most law-firm professional liability policies are reported to be silent on AI, but broad exclusions exist at some carriers, and an ISO general-liability exclusion took effect 1 January 2026 (IAMAG-LAWFIRM-2026; OLSHAN-AIEXCL-2026; INSINSIDER-LAWYERS-2026).
- **Governance is uneven by firm size.** In ILTA's 2026 survey, a formal GenAI policy is reported by 57% of firms under 50 lawyers and 98% of firms with 350-699 lawyers; training before access is required by 11% and 72% in the smallest and largest bands (ILTA-TECHSURVEY-2026, Q3.18 table, p.5). Thomson Reuters finds 34% of professionals use AI their organisation has not sanctioned (TR-FOP-2026, key findings).

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 US bar guidance: the common baseline

**ABA Formal Opinion 512 (29 July 2024).** The ABA PDF was blocked (403). The content below comes from a plain-English summary on Ezel that links to the opinion (secondary), cross-checked against the quotations in Texas Opinion 705 and the New York City Bar opinion.
- The opinion applies existing Model Rules 1.1, 1.4, 1.5, 1.6, 3.1, 3.3, 5.1, 5.3 and 8.4(c) to generative AI rather than creating new rules (EZEL-ABA512-2024, "Plain-English summary" and "Background").
- Lawyers need not become AI experts but need a reasonable understanding of a tool's capabilities and limits, and uncritical reliance on output can violate the competence rule (EZEL-ABA512-2024, summary, Rule 1.1 paragraph).
- Before inputting information relating to a representation into a self-learning tool, the lawyer must obtain informed consent; general boilerplate in an engagement letter is not sufficient (EZEL-ABA512-2024, Rule 1.6 paragraph). Texas Opinion 705 reads the ABA opinion as going "a step further" than California's 2023 guidance by suggesting informed consent (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Confidentiality).
- Disclosure of AI use to the client is fact-specific: required if the client asks, the engagement agreement requires it, consent is needed, the fee is affected, or the output influences a significant decision (EZEL-ABA512-2024, Rule 1.4 paragraph).
- Managerial and supervisory lawyers must set clear policies, provide training and oversee outside providers (EZEL-ABA512-2024, supervision sentence).
- The Ezel page was written by Ezel, not the ABA; the ABA text is the authority (EZEL-ABA512-2024, disclaimer).

**California, 2026 revision.** State Bar of California, Standing Committee on Professional Responsibility and Conduct (COPRAC).
- The Board of Trustees approved the revised Practical Guidance on 14 May 2026; the original was published 16 November 2023 (CALBAR-ETHTECH-2026, "Additional resources"). The guidance replaces the 2023 version and, at the California Supreme Court's request, addresses agentic AI (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Exec. Summary p.1).
- Competence has two parts: baseline understanding of the system's capabilities, data sources and limits, and independent review, verification and correction of outputs. Competence "requires more than" detecting hallucinations (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Duties of Competence and Diligence, p.4).
- "The greater the level of system autonomy, the greater the lawyer's obligation to implement oversight mechanisms" (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.5, 18 words). The duty includes periodic reassessment as models change (p.5).
- Lawyers must not deploy agentic systems so that they make substantive legal determinations, communicate legal advice, prepare and file pleadings or act in a representative capacity without meaningful lawyer supervision (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Exec. Summary p.2). No document may go to a court without lawyer review and approval, and AI may not autonomously file or communicate with a court (p.9).
- Agentic systems with access to email, document management, client files or calendars raise "acute" confidentiality questions; a lawyer must not permit autonomous external transmission of client information without safeguards and human review (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Duty of Confidentiality, p.5-6).
- Managerial lawyers should set clear policies including for agentic tools, train lawyers and nonlawyer staff periodically, and update policies as systems become more autonomous (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Duty to Supervise, p.7).
- A lawyer must follow client instructions or guidelines that restrict AI use (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Communication, p.7).
- On bias, the guidance names AI used for employment actions or for screening clients or candidates as a risk, and says firms should establish policies to identify and address bias (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Prohibition on Discrimination, p.9).

**Texas Opinion 705 (February 2025).**
- Rule 1.01 "almost certainly" does not require use of generative AI, but a lawyer who uses it needs a reasonable and current understanding (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Competence).
- Confidentiality precautions drawn from the committee's cloud-computing opinion: understand the technology, review and possibly renegotiate terms of service, learn the tool's data-security protections, and train lawyers and staff (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Confidentiality, list items 1-4).
- Verification is non-negotiable: lawyers are responsible for work product "regardless of who (or what)" does the first draft (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Oversight/Supervision).

**DC Ethics Opinion 388 (2024; month not shown on the page).**
- Applies Rules 1.6, 5.1 and 5.3 and the duty of candor; says a lawyer billing hourly may bill only for time actually spent even if AI shortens it (DCBAR-OP388-2024, opening summary paragraph).
- Says clients "typically should not be asked to consent" to wide-ranging disclosures; this is reported by a secondary source and was not located in the opinion text I read (HAQQ-CONFID-2026, comparison of six opinions). Treat as unverified.

**New York City Bar Formal Opinion 2024-5 (7 August 2024).**
- Defines "law firm" to include corporations' and other entities' legal departments, so the opinion reaches **in-house** teams (NYCBAR-OP2024-5, fn 8).
- A lawyer must not input confidential client information into a system lacking adequate confidentiality and security protections without informed consent, and must watch ethical walls even in closed systems (NYCBAR-OP2024-5, Confidentiality row).
- A lawyer using an AI chatbot for client intake must adequately supervise it (NYCBAR-OP2024-5, supervision section).

**Other US state bodies.** Florida Opinion 24-1 is cited by Texas and DC for "efficiency must not result in falsely inflated claims of time" (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Fees). I could not open the Florida page text. A one-off compendium produced by an AI agent and published on LawSites lists further states, but its author states it was not verified, so it is not used (LAWSITES-COMPENDIUM-2025, introduction).

### 2.2 UK and other regulators

**Law Society of England and Wales, "Generative AI - the essentials" (undated; mentions events up to April 2026).**
- There is no AI-specific regulation in the UK and no current plan to introduce legislation (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026, "What is the issue?").
- The SRA "does not have specific guidance" on generative AI related to use or disclosure of use for client care, and there is no SRA or HMCTS guidance on AI in court documents; the page tells solicitors and clients to decide whether and how tools are used (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026, "Things to consider and risk management"). The SRA's own pages were blocked by a bot challenge, so this is the Law Society's characterisation.
- A checklist covers vendor data management, whether the tool is a closed system, documenting inputs, outputs and errors, reviewing outputs, and establishing "the liability and insurance coverage" for AI use (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026, checklist). It advises negotiating warranties, indemnities and liability limits with vendors (procurement section).
- A Law Society report published April 2026 found no evidence that agentic AI is currently used in legal practice (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026, agentic AI section). Compare with California's agentic rules (2.1) and vendor claims in the landscape; this is a live disagreement about how far agentic use has actually spread.
- Aspects of the guidance are said to be relevant to solicitors working **in-house** (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026, introduction).

**Bar Standards Board.** On 18 May 2026 the BSB published guidance on safe use of AI and emerging technologies, citing "recent cases" and the *Ayinde* judgment; it explains how existing BSB Handbook duties apply and lists good-practice principles including transparency, confidentiality and data governance (BSB-PR-2026). The Law360 headline "absolute caution" in the landscape was not opened and is not relied on.

**Courts as regulators (England and Wales).**
- *Ayinde v Haringey / Al-Haroun v Qatar National Bank* [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), 6 June 2025: the Divisional Court said those with leadership responsibilities (heads of chambers, managing partners) and regulators must take "practical and effective measures", and that in future Hamid hearings the profession can expect the court to inquire whether those responsibilities were fulfilled (EWHC-AYINDE-2025, para 9).
- The court's powers listed include public admonition, costs and wasted costs orders, striking out, referral to a regulator, contempt proceedings and referral to the police (EWHC-AYINDE-2025, para 23). It said placing false material before the court is likely to merit a regulator referral and that admonition alone is unlikely to suffice save in exceptional circumstances (paras 29-31).
- *Rodney v Gee'z Micro Bar & Pitstop* (County Court at Dudley, 23 April 2026, HHJ Grimshaw): a solicitor and the firm's director were referred to the SRA after filings with mis-cited authorities. The judge found the errors due to "reckless disregard" for accuracy rather than a deliberate attempt to mislead, declined contempt proceedings, and found "failures ... on the part of both solicitors and the management of the firm" (CCDUDLEY-RODNEY-2026, paras 56-58). The solicitors had said supervision was strengthened and had taken a generative-AI course shortly before the hearing (paras 31, 34, 56).
- Civil Justice Council working group (set up January 2025): its June 2026 update says respondents largely agree no additional AI-specific requirements are needed for pleadings and skeleton arguments, with witness statements and expert evidence still under consideration; the direction of travel is to keep the current position for professional drafting, introduce "proportionate transparency" for expert evidence, and report later in 2026 (CJC-AI-2026, June 2026 update). The consultation paper proposed declarations only where AI is used to generate evidence the court is asked to rely on (CJC-INTERIM-2026, para 1.4).
- Judicial guidance (October 2025), quoted in the CJC paper, says there is "no reason" a legal representative ought to refer to AI use if used responsibly, depending on context (CJC-INTERIM-2026, paras 2.5-2.6).

### 2.3 Accounting bodies

- **ACCA, "Navigating ethics for trust" (14 October 2026).** Based on 1,178 responses from finance and accountancy professionals in 135 countries: 61% cite AI use as the leading ethical challenge, up from 32% in 2024; 72% say AI tools enhance the quality and efficiency of their work; 50% say ethical challenges have become harder to resolve over three years; 89% believe they have the skills to navigate them (ACCA-ETHICS-2026, "Key questions" 2-4). Listed AI ethics issues include overreliance, verification, confidentiality, unclear accountability and weak organisational guardrails (question 4).
- **IAASB.** In June 2025 the IAASB launched a Technology Quality Management initiative on how AI affects application of ISQM 1, ISQM 2 and ISA 220 (Revised). Roundtables in late 2025 engaged more than 240 stakeholders; participants broadly agreed the quality-management standards are a strong foundation but asked for practical guidance. In December 2025 the board approved developing non-authoritative material (IAASB-TECHCAT-2026, "Technology Quality Management"). Routes to assurance; shown here because firm-level quality management is the standard-setter's chosen control point.
- **UK Financial Reporting Council (March 2026).** A trade report says the FRC issued guidance on generative and agentic AI in audit, identifies three failure routes (flawed input or model, misinterpretation, and too little work performed), and quotes its standards director: "You can't blame it on the box" (RESULTSENSE-FRC-2026, body; secondary, citing the FT). The landscape's check of the FRC news page found no AI item, so the primary document was not confirmed. Routes to assurance.
- **AICPA and CIMA.** The AICPA AI landing page returned "page can't be found" through the mirror, and no AICPA or IESBA AI guidance text was read (see Gaps).
- **Case with a consulting/advisory reading.** Deloitte Australia agreed to repay the final instalment of a $440,000 contract (Australian government) after errors, including non-existent references, were found in a government review report; the revised report discloses use of Azure OpenAI GPT-4o on the department's tenancy, and Deloitte did not say AI caused the errors (GUARDIAN-DELOITTE-2025, body). Deloitte said the matter "has been resolved directly with the client" and that the updates do not affect findings or recommendations (same).

### 2.4 Consulting, engineering and architecture codes

- **ICMCI Code of Responsible Use of AI for the Management Consulting Profession, v1.0 (adopted May 2026; launched 4 June 2026).** Seven principles: human responsibility and oversight; fairness; transparency; privacy and confidentiality; safety and robustness; accountability; sustainability (ICMCI-AICODE-2026, Foreword; ICMCI-NEWS-2026).
  - Use of AI "does not reduce, transfer or extinguish" accountability (s.2.2). Misuse is a breach comparable to traditional misconduct, including submitting unreviewed outputs and entering confidential client data in breach of obligations (s.2.3).
  - For individual certified consultants the principles are mandatory; firms and member institutes are "expected to align" proportionately (s.3 intro).
  - Clients must be informed when AI tools materially contribute to analysis, recommendations or deliverables; routine productivity tools need not be individually disclosed (s.3.3). Clients must be told when AI materially affects advice, the role of the tools, the key risks, and how client data is handled (s.7.1). Explicit client agreement is expected for personal data, profiling, automated decision-making or other high-impact uses (s.7.2). Firms are "encouraged" to address AI scope in engagement terms (s.7.3).
  - Consultants must comply with client instructions restricting AI tools (s.5.4) and independently verify facts, calculations, citations and references (s.5.1).
  - Firms should document significant AI-supported decisions proportionately (s.3.6). Enforcement mechanics sit with member institutes (s.10, not read in full).
- **NSPE Board of Ethical Review Case 24-2, "Use of Artificial Intelligence in Engineering Practice".** Using AI drafting tools is not unethical per se, but sealing work without keeping responsible charge over the tool and its output was unethical; there is no ethical duty to disclose AI use to the client unless the contract requires it, though transparency is favoured when AI plays a substantial role (NSPE-BER24-2, Conclusions 2-3, p.8). The case notes the facts may be real or hypothetical (p.9). Date: the case number indicates 2024; the PDF file is dated August 2025.
- **ACEC AI Risk Subcommittee guidelines (July 2024).** Suggested practices for design-professional firms: board-level or governance-group authorisation of AI policies, approved-tool lists, controls on posting client information to unapproved AI services, and documenting sources of generated content. The document says it is not a standard of care (ACEC-AIGUIDE-2024, "Guideline Usage", "Governance", "Sources and Tools").

### 2.5 Confidentiality and privilege

- ***United States v. Heppner*, S.D.N.Y. (Rakoff J).** A senior executive indicted for securities fraud used Claude on his own initiative; about 31 documents seized from his home memorialised the exchanges. The court held they were neither privileged nor work product (ORRICK-HEPPNER-2026, "The Case" and "Key Holdings"). Reasons: an AI tool is not an attorney; no reasonable expectation of confidentiality given the consumer privacy policy; and no counsel direction for work product (same). Date conflict: Orrick says the opinion issued 13 February 2026; the HAQQ blog cites 17 February 2026 (HAQQ-CONFID-2026, sources list). Not resolved.
- Orrick says enterprise AI platforms with negotiated confidentiality terms "may present a different picture, though this has not been tested in court" (ORRICK-HEPPNER-2026, Key Takeaways).
- ***Warner v. Gilbarco* (E.D. Mich.).** A self-represented litigant's chatbot communications were held protected as work product, with the court treating AI programs as "tools"; the contrast with *Heppner* turns on the litigant's status and on how the court viewed the platform (BROWNING-ARMFOR-2026, "Recent Conflicting Judicial Rulings" slide). Secondary summary of a conference handout.
- ***Morgan v. V2X* (D. Colo., 30 March 2026).** Reported to have amended a protective order so no party may enter confidential material into an AI platform unless the provider is contractually barred from training on or disclosing inputs, with written documentation retained (HAQQ-CONFID-2026, protective-order paragraph). Secondary; the order was not read.
- **In-house view.** In an ACC global survey reported in September 2024, 56% of respondents believed using AI tools could compromise lawyer-client privilege (LEXPERT-ACC-2024). Older than the 12-18 month window; shown as the only in-house privilege measure found. **in-house**
- **Practical consequence for non-lawyer advisers.** I found no ruling on whether consulting or accounting advisers' AI chats are protected. This is a gap, not a finding.

### 2.6 Client AI terms: outside-counsel guidelines and procurement

- **Surveys of buyers.** KPMG's 2026 Global General Counsel Outlook (468 senior legal leaders in 28 jurisdictions): 82% agree or strongly agree they expect their firms to track and share their AI use in client matters; the figure appears in an Above the Law summary and I did not find it on KPMG's own pages (ATL-STATS-2026; KPMG-GCOUTLOOK-2026, page header for the sample). **in-house**
- **What guidelines say.** Zscaler's guidelines (revised 7 April 2025) bar its confidential information from being used to train AI tools; the FDIC's Outside Counsel Deskbook (revised April 2026) requires written notice before using generative AI on an FDIC matter and bars entering sensitive information into open-environment tools (HAQQ-CONFID-2026, outside-counsel guidelines paragraph). Secondary; neither document was opened.
- **How common.** A Fulkerson Advisors study dated 16 September 2026 searched 1,054 published outside-counsel guidelines: 20 mention AI, 12 of those require disclosure of AI use, and 9 require keeping client information out of public AI tools; HAQQ calculates 0.9% (2024), 2.2% (2025) and 3.8% (2026) of dated guidelines mention AI (HAQQ-CONFID-2026, same paragraph). This is one consultancy's sample, skewed to public bodies and universities by its own description, and I did not see the study.
- **Panel evidence from Legalweek.** Clients ask for line-item transparency on invoices (was AI used, how much did it save), while "other clients are issuing guidelines stating they won't pay for certain services if performed by AI" (TRI-LEGALWEEK-2026, billing discussion). Speakers said firms often answer "We're still gathering the data" (same).
- **Government procurement.** After errors in Deloitte reports for the Newfoundland and Labrador and Australian governments, Canada's Public Services and Procurement Canada said it "now requires suppliers to identify any use of artificial intelligence in advance", and a federal department said contracts with third parties preparing reports include conditions on AI use (CP-DELOITTECANADA-2025). The Open Contracting Partnership argued the Australian case shows the need for transparency on how and when AI is used in government contracting (ACCTIMES-OCP-2025). I did not find a published Australian contract clause.
- **Rule side.** California tells lawyers to follow client AI instructions (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.7); the ICMCI code does the same for consultants (ICMCI-AICODE-2026, s.5.4).

### 2.7 Supervision and firm governance

- **What firms report.** ILTA's 2026 Technology Survey (508 firms) shows the policy gradient below. Columns are firm size by number of lawyers (ILTA-TECHSURVEY-2026, Q3.18 table, p.5).

| Policy element | Under 50 | 50-149 | 150-349 | 350-699 | Over 700 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal policy in place | 57% | 80% | 85% | 98% | 91% |
| Work product must be reviewed by a human | 31% | 59% | 69% | 83% | 79% |
| Specific training required before use | 11% | 30% | 47% | 63% | 72% |
| Outputs must be cited / independently verified | 25% | 42% | 40% | 60% | 55% |
| Must obtain client approval | 8% | 20% | 24% | 38% | 34% |
| Prohibit GenAI for client work | 7% | 2% | 3% | 2% | 0% |

  The survey also reports that Copilot and Teams Premium deployments tripled in two years and that GenAI capabilities are being activated through collaboration tools "without discrete deployment events" (ILTA-TECHSURVEY-2026, p.4). ILTA covers law firms only.
- **Shadow use.** Thomson Reuters' 2026 survey (1,816 professionals, 62 countries, March-April 2026) finds 34% use AI tools their organisation has not sanctioned and 41% of professionals who use AI at work lack access to tools meeting the survey's "fiduciary-grade" conditions; professionals say tools must safeguard confidential data (96%), ground outputs in authoritative content (94%) and give explainable reasoning (90%) (TR-FOP-2026, key findings and "Fiduciary-grade" section). Vendor-sponsored research.
- **What supervision looked like when it failed.** In *Johnson v. Dunn* (N.D. Ala., 23 July 2025) three Butler Snow lawyers were removed from the case, reprimanded, referred to the Alabama State Bar and ordered to disclose the order to clients and courts; the judge did not sanction the firm, saying it "acted reasonably in its efforts to prevent this misconduct" (ABAJ-JOHNSON-2025). The firm said the AI use violated firm policy and engaged Morgan Lewis to review its filings: 28 lawyers reviewed more than 2,400 citations across 330 filings and found no other incorrect case references (same). In April 2026 Sullivan & Cromwell, which holds an enterprise ChatGPT licence, said firm policy requiring training was not followed and that a secondary review missed the errors; this comes from a trade blog whose reliability is mixed, so only the apology and error ledger are taken from the ABA Journal (WEBPRONEWS-SC-2026, para 3; ABAJ-SC-2026).
- **Supervision of other people's AI.** In the K&L Gates matter, associates at one firm incorporated an outline from a second firm without checking it or asking whether AI had been used; a special master found about 9 of 27 citations incorrect and at least two authorities non-existent (ABAJ-KLGATES-2025).
- **Duty to detect others' errors.** A California appellate court declined to award opposing counsel fees against a lawyer whose brief had fabricated quotations because opposing counsel had not alerted the court; a commentator reads this as a possible emerging competence standard (LAWSITES-NOLAND-2025). Commentary, not a holding.

### 2.8 Billing ethics for AI-assisted work

- **Hourly.** A lawyer may bill actual time spent prompting and reviewing AI work but must not bill hours AI saved: California (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, Charging for Work, p.8), Texas (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Fees, quoting DC Opinion 267 and ABA Opinion 93-379), DC (DCBAR-OP388-2024, opening summary) and the ABA (EZEL-ABA512-2024, fees paragraph).
- **Alternative fees.** California says lawyers may use flat fees and build AI time savings into competitive rates, with fee agreements making clear how fees and costs are calculated and how far AI factors into rates (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.8).
- **Overhead versus pass-through.** Subscriptions for AI with general office functionality are typically overhead and not separately chargeable; per-use or matter-specific AI costs can be charged if the fee agreement discloses it, charges reflect actual cost, and no markup is added without informed written consent (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.8). Texas says per-use fees may be recoverable only up to the amount actually incurred (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Fees). The ABA opinion is summarised as treating overhead tools as non-billable and reasonable per-use costs as passable (EZEL-ABA512-2024, "In practice").
- **Value tension.** A panelist at Legalweek 2026 (Zarwin Baum) raised that compressed hours make an itemised hourly bill undersell delivered value and floated a return to a single services-rendered line, then asked whether clients would accept it; the article leaves that open (TRI-LEGALWEEK-2026, billing discussion).
- **Consulting.** The ICMCI code does not set a billing rule; it covers disclosure and engagement-letter terms (ICMCI-AICODE-2026, s.7). I found no consulting-body billing rule for AI-assisted work (see Gaps).

### 2.9 Liability and insurance

- **Policy wording.** Effective 1 January 2026, ISO introduced three generative-AI endorsements for commercial general liability policies using "arising out of" wording; W.R. Berkley is reported to have introduced "absolute" AI exclusions in D&O, E&O and fiduciary policies (OLSHAN-AIEXCL-2026, ISO and "Other Policy Types" sections). The author (a policyholder-side lawyer) says an insurer may argue that a professional who relied on unverified AI output never rendered a "professional service" (same).
- **Law-firm market.** A broker told IA Magazine in March 2026 that the vast majority of law-firm professional liability policies have no express AI exclusion and expects firms without documented AI protocols to fall out of preferred-risk tiers over 12 to 18 months; renewal questions on AI are described as largely check-the-box (IAMAG-LAWFIRM-2026).
- **Insurer view.** Insurance Insider (13 May 2026, partly paywalled) reports brokers seeing "sweeping" AI exclusions on some lawyers' E&O policies, most E&O still silent, sub-limits emerging as affirmative cover, and Hamilton and Berkley named as leaders on broad exclusions. It reports that legal AI vendors transfer liability to the deploying firm, per an MGA called Testudo, which tracks 720 US incidents of attorneys sanctioned or disciplined for hallucinated content, with a highest recorded fine of $110,000 (INSINSIDER-LAWYERS-2026). That tracker's definition differs from the Charlotin database, so the figures do not compare.
- **Guidance to firms.** The Law Society tells solicitors to check liability and insurance cover for AI use (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026, checklist and risk section). A Jenner & Block partner's February 2026 article says insurers are adding generative-AI exclusions across CGL, D&O and professional liability lines (JENNER-KROEGER-2026; search-listing summary of a Law360 article, body not read).
- **Architects and engineers.** Insurance Insider reports brokers also seeing broad AI exclusions on architects' policies (INSINSIDER-LAWYERS-2026).

### 2.10 Sanctions, discipline and court responses

- **Scale.** Charlotin's database lists 2,202 decisions as of 19 October 2026: 1,501 USA, 234 Canada, 112 Australia, 76 UK. By party: 872 lawyers, 1,267 self-represented litigants, 32 judges, 17 experts. By type of error: 1,806 fabricated, 587 false quotes, 933 misrepresented. The page counts decisions where a court found or implied reliance on hallucinated content, and its filter counts can overlap (CHARLOTIN-DB-2026, header and filter lists).
- **Growth.** An earlier snapshot reported 2,077 decisions on 23 September 2026 (HAQQ-CONFID-2026, opening note), so the database recorded 125 more by 19 October. Part of this is lag in finding cases, not necessarily a rise in filings.
- **Amounts.** Largest sanction cited in a 2026 conference handout: nearly $86,000 in *ByoPlanet v. Johansson & Gilstrap* (S.D. Fla., August 2025); a $10,000 sanction in *Noland v. Land of the Free* (Cal. App., September 2025), after which the State Bar Court recommended an agreed one-year suspension in February 2026; $15,000 each in *Whiting v. City of Athens* (6th Cir., 13 March 2026); a federal-court suspension of one year in *Clark Pear v. MVP Realty* (M.D. Fla., 2025) (BROWNING-ARMFOR-2026, "Lawyers Behaving Badly" slides). Insurance Insider's highest recorded fine is $110,000 (INSINSIDER-LAWYERS-2026).
- **Repeat firms.** The same handout says Gordon Rees faced sanctions again in *Huynh v. Redis Labs* after an August 2025 sanction in a bankruptcy case (BROWNING-ARMFOR-2026, item 4).
- **Large-firm incidents.** K&L Gates and Ellis George: $31,100 jointly and severally, 6 May 2025 (ABAJ-KLGATES-2025). Latham & Watkins: admitted a fabricated article title and authors in an expert-report footnote (ABAJ-BUTLER-2025). Sullivan & Cromwell: apology letter of 18 April 2026 listing about three dozen errors, some of which the firm said were clerical and not AI-related (ABAJ-SC-2026). Richards, Layton & Finger: affidavits asking the Delaware Chancery Court not to sanction it, reported July 2026, paywalled (BLOOMBERGLAW-RLF-2026).
- **Tool reliability evidence.** A preregistered Stanford evaluation of legal research tools found Lexis+ AI and Westlaw's AI products each hallucinated between 17% and 33% of the time on its test set, against vendor claims of "hallucination-free" output (STANFORD-MAGESH-2024, abstract). Measured by the study's own typology; 2024 versions of the tools.
- **Discipline beyond fines.** California's State Bar Court recommended a one-year suspension in the Mostafavi matter (BROWNING-ARMFOR-2026, item 3). Other reported suspensions (for example a Nebraska Supreme Court suspension in April 2026) were seen only in secondary or blog summaries and are not relied on here (search-only).
- **UK.** *Ayinde* and *Rodney* (2.2). The Law Society lists the sanction range and says the court expects severe sanctions other than in exceptional circumstances (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026, court-papers section).

### 2.11 EU AI Act

The AI Act became applicable on 2 August 2026, with AI literacy obligations applying from 2 February 2025; high-risk uses in areas including employment apply from 2 December 2027 under the AI Omnibus, which entered into force 27 July 2026 (EC-AIACT-2026, "Timeline" paragraphs). For firms serving EU clients, AI used in recruitment, staffing or performance decisions is the exposure the Commission page names; professional-conduct duties sit alongside this regime. California and ICMCI flag the same employment-bias risk (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.9; ICMCI-AICODE-2026, s.3.2).

## 3. What it means for an AI transformation conversation

### 3.1 Implications

1. **Guardrails are a design input, not a brake.** Every regulator text read keeps accountability with the professional and scales required oversight to autonomy (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.5; ICMCI-AICODE-2026, s.2.2; EWHC-AYINDE-2025, para 9). Workflow redesign (agents, review gates) therefore has to name who reviews, how, and how that is evidenced.
2. **Leadership is now named in the standard.** English courts say they will ask whether leaders fulfilled their duties (EWHC-AYINDE-2025, para 9); California tells managers to set policy, train and update (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.7). In *Johnson v. Dunn* the judge said the firm "acted reasonably in its efforts to prevent this misconduct" and did not sanction it (ABAJ-JOHNSON-2025). An advisor can treat the AI policy and training record as legal protection as well as hygiene.
3. **Firm size predicts governance gaps.** The smallest firms report lowest policy and training coverage (ILTA-TECHSURVEY-2026, p.5). Unsanctioned use is at one in three professionals (TR-FOP-2026). Opening question: what runs outside the approved stack?
4. **Pricing models and ethics rules meet.** Hourly billing cannot capture AI savings (2.8). Flat fee, subscription or outcome pricing sidestep the rule but California still requires fee agreements to explain how AI factors in (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.8). A pricing change is therefore also an engagement-letter change.
5. **Client terms are the next constraint, and visibility is poor.** Buyers expect disclosure (ATL-STATS-2026) and some are writing restrictions (HAQQ-CONFID-2026) while the measured share of published guidelines mentioning AI is small. Firms should expect to answer AI questions in RFPs and engagement terms before they are common in written guidelines.
6. **Confidentiality design differs by tier.** *Heppner* turned on consumer terms and on the tool not being a lawyer (ORRICK-HEPPNER-2026). Account tier and contract terms belong in the transformation plan.
7. **Insurance and incident evidence will shape the business case.** Silent cover may not last (INSINSIDER-LAWYERS-2026); documented protocols may affect terms (IAMAG-LAWFIRM-2026).
8. **Consulting and engineering have fewer binding texts.** The ICMCI code is mandatory for individual certified consultants only (ICMCI-AICODE-2026, s.3 intro); NSPE is guidance on a real-or-hypothetical case (NSPE-BER24-2, p.9). The Deloitte cases show the exposure is commercial and procurement-led (refund, supplier disclosure requirement) more than disciplinary (GUARDIAN-DELOITTE-2025; CP-DELOITTECANADA-2025).

### 3.2 Interview probes

1. Who in the firm owns AI policy, and when did it last change? Was it updated for agents that act (send, file, read email) and not only for chat tools? (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.5-7)
2. For your last five AI-assisted client deliverables, can you show who reviewed what, and what they checked? What would that evidence look like to a court or a client auditor? (EWHC-AYINDE-2025, para 9)
3. Which AI tools are used on client matters that IT has not approved? How would you find out? (TR-FOP-2026: 34%)
4. How do your engagement letters and RFP responses describe AI use today? Have any clients restricted it, asked for line-item AI time, or asked to see your usage? (TRI-LEGALWEEK-2026; ATL-STATS-2026)
5. If AI cuts the hours on an hourly matter, what happens to the bill? Is the next fee model written into the engagement terms? (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.8)
6. Have you read your professional liability policy for AI exclusions, and what did the broker say at renewal? (OLSHAN-AIEXCL-2026; IAMAG-LAWFIRM-2026)
7. For in-house or shared-service teams: does outside counsel disclose AI use to you, and do your guidelines say what they may do? (ATL-STATS-2026) **in-house**
8. Where staff share a client's confidential data with a vendor tool, which account tier and contract terms apply? (ORRICK-HEPPNER-2026; CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.5)

### 3.3 Objections and evidence-based responses

| Objection | Response from the evidence |
|---|---|
| "The rules ban this, so we should wait." | None of the texts read ban use. They require competence, verification, supervision and confidentiality (CALBAR-GENAI-2026; TEXASBAR-OP705-2025; EZEL-ABA512-2024). Texas says the rules likely do not require AI use either, and warns against unnecessarily retreating from useful technology (TEXASBAR-OP705-2025, Competence). |
| "We have a policy, so we are covered." | The *Johnson v. Dunn* firm said the use violated its policy, and three lawyers were still sanctioned; the firm itself was spared, citing its precautions (ABAJ-JOHNSON-2025). Policies protect the firm only if they are operated and evidenced. |
| "Our legal research tool is hallucination-free." | A preregistered study found 17-33% hallucination rates for two vendors' tools in 2024 on its test set (STANFORD-MAGESH-2024). Versions have since changed; verification duties have not (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.4). |
| "Clients are not asking about AI." | In the KPMG survey 82% of GCs expect firms to track and share AI use (ATL-STATS-2026). In one count, 20 of 1,054 published outside-counsel guidelines (1.9%) mention AI, and 3.8% of those dated 2026 by HAQQ's arithmetic (HAQQ-CONFID-2026). Both can be true: expectation runs ahead of written terms. |
| "AI savings are ours to keep under hourly billing." | Bar opinions say hourly billers charge for time actually spent (2.8). Savings can be kept through fee structures agreed in advance (CALBAR-GENAI-2026, p.8). Some clients say they will not pay for work done by AI (TRI-LEGALWEEK-2026). |
| "Our insurer will cover mistakes." | Most policies are reported silent, but exclusions exist and an insurer might argue unverified output is not a professional service (IAMAG-LAWFIRM-2026; OLSHAN-AIEXCL-2026; INSINSIDER-LAWYERS-2026). Check the wording. |
| "Private AI keeps everything privileged." | *Heppner* concerned a consumer tool and a non-lawyer acting alone; whether enterprise terms change the result is untested (ORRICK-HEPPNER-2026). Another court protected a self-represented litigant's chatbot use (BROWNING-ARMFOR-2026). Treat privilege as unsettled. |
| "Agents are not in legal practice yet." | The Law Society's April 2026 report found no evidence of current agentic use in legal practice (LAWSOC-ESSENTIALS-2026), yet California and the Bar Standards Board have already written agentic rules (CALBAR-GENAI-2026; BSB-PR-2026). Writing controls before deployment is the point. |
| "Consulting has no professional rules for this." | ICMCI's code is mandatory for individual CMC holders and expects firms to align (ICMCI-AICODE-2026, s.3). Government buyers are adding supplier disclosure (CP-DELOITTECANADA-2025). |
